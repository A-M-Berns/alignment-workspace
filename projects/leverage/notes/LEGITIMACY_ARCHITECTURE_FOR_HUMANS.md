# The legitimacy architecture, in plain terms

Status: `ci-only`. Names are provisional.

## The situation

Imagine a reasoner that has to keep changing its mind — not only about facts, but
about which considerations count, what its words mean, and what it owes an answer
to. You want to say something about whether it is doing this honestly. You cannot
check its conclusions against the truth, because the questions it is revising are
exactly the ones that do not settle.

So you check its record instead. Did each change have a reason behind it that was
already on the table? And did every question it owed an answer to either get
answered, get explicitly written off by someone entitled to write it off, or stay
on the books?

Those two questions are the architecture. The first is about single steps; call
it responsiveness. The second is about the whole history; call it answerability.

## What the two questions buy

Quite a lot, and it is worth being concrete about what.

**A question cannot be made to disappear by changing the subject.** If the
reasoner retires the entire vocabulary a demand was stated in — stops talking
about harm and starts talking about welfare — the demand survives, because the
demand's identity was never a word. It has to be answered, or split into new
questions that inherit it, or written off with a signature and a public note.
That is checked here over every three-step pattern of dispositions, and it fails
in the right way when the signatures are removed.

**The reasoner may still change radically.** This is the part people expect to
lose and do not. A trajectory that abandons its old concepts, rewrites its own
standard for what counts as a reason, and reverses its verdict passes both
questions — and the verdict it ends with is one its starting self would have
refused. That has to be allowed, or the framework forbids exactly the learning it
is supposed to describe.

**So what does the starting self get?** Not agreement. It gets an audit trail:
for every question it cared about, a determinate answer to "what happened to
this?", with the paperwork attached, readable at the end without re-running the
history. That is a weaker thing than endorsement and a more useful one — trust in
a process rather than agreement with a conclusion.

## What the two questions do not buy

Three failures, and they are not repairable by tightening the definitions.

**Nobody is checking what goes on the record.** Both questions ask what the
reasoner does with the reasons available to it. Neither asks where those reasons
came from. So the reasoner can file the permission slip it needs and then use it,
and every check passes. In the model this takes one extra step: a move that gets
refused for lack of authority is accepted, and the whole run counts as legitimate,
once the reasoner writes its own authority note first.

**Nobody is checking what never came up.** A demand that was never docketed is
not on the record, and both questions are about the record. An advisor who
controls which issues become visible faces no obstacle at all. This is not an
oversight to be patched: you cannot write a rule about the record that quantifies
over what is missing from it. It needs a separate condition, comparing the
demands that arise against the demands that get filed.

**Nobody can check influence from the record, ever.** This is the sharp one, and
it is not this round's result — it comes from the deference line, where it is
proved in Lean. Take a reasoner who genuinely works something out, and a reasoner
who has simply adopted an advisor's opinion, and arrange the advisor's opinions to
match. The two write *identical records*. Every property of the record is the same
for both. So any honest-bookkeeping test — including this one — calls them both
clean, while one of them has been steered from a conclusion of one quarter to a
conclusion of three quarters. Here that is exhibited inside the normative record
itself: same paperwork, influence gap of exactly one half.

The consequence for the deference project is worth stating flatly. Clean books do
not rule out manipulation, and no amount of better bookkeeping will. What clean
books rule out is the *other* family of manipulations — the ones where the
paperwork is the trick — and that family turns out to be well covered.

## The learning question

The natural next step is to ask whether the reasoner improves. The standard tool
compares it against a family of alternative strategies and asks whether it does
nearly as well as the best of them. The family has to consist of alternatives
that would themselves have been legitimate — otherwise you are measuring the
reasoner against cheating.

The obvious family is: rules that turn any permitted response into another
permitted response, at every point in the history. This round computes that
family exactly, and it has an unfortunate property. Each response has to be sent
somewhere permitted at every moment where it was permitted — so it must land in
the intersection of all those moments' permissions. If the reasoner's situation
changes enough, those intersections shrink to single points, the only surviving
rule is "change nothing", and the comparison becomes empty. On a four-response
example this happens exactly: the family has one member.

Which means the comparison family has to depend on the situation, and then
checking that a member of it is legitimate means re-running the legitimacy check
everywhere it might be used. That is the same obstacle an earlier round hit from
the other side, and the reorganization has made it easier to state without making
it go away.

## The verdict

Two questions, well posed, with a real conservation law behind the second and a
real permission for radical change behind the pair. Two more questions needed
that nobody had separated out. One impossibility inherited, which decides what
this can ever contribute to the manipulation problem. And one negative result
about the learning comparison that says the current shape of that target is the
wrong shape.

The names changed less than the picture did.
