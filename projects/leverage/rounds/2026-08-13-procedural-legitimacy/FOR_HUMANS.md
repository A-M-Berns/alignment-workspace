# What this round did

The last round found that two conditions on a reasoner's history — every step
licensed by reasons already on the table, every obligation accounted for — were
not enough. Two more were proposed: say where a reason's *authority* came from,
and say which questions the reasoner was obliged to face. This round asked
whether all four together finally pin down what it means for a reasoner's
procedure to be honest.

They do not. And the interesting part is why not.

## First, what the new conditions genuinely buy

**Authority cannot be manufactured, however long the chain.** The rule that makes
this work is a split between two things that were being run together. What a
reason *says* is free — a reasoner must be able to think new thoughts, prove new
things, invent concepts, and produce genuine objections. What a reason is
*entitled to decide* is not free: a conclusion inherits jurisdiction from its
premises and can never have more. So you can derive anything and authorise
nothing. Four links or four hundred, the end of the chain cannot decide something
the beginning could not.

Circular authorisation — two reasons each licensed by the other — falls out the
same way, and so does the one act genuinely closed to the reasoner: writing down
its own founding permission.

**Questions cannot be un-asked after the fact.** Whether a question is owed an
answer is settled when the question comes up, not when the answer falls due. That
single choice defeats the trick of noticing an inconvenient question and then
revising the rule that says which questions matter. Read the rule at the later
moment instead and the same trick works — the test shows both.

**Flooding the docket doesn't help.** If service obligations are real obligations
with deadlines, burying one question under a thousand others means missing the
deadlines, which makes the flooder illegitimate. It is not an evasion; it is a
confession.

## And a correction to the previous round

Last round's conservation result gave each obligation a single fate — live,
discharged, written off. That is fine until an obligation splits in two and the
halves end differently. Then one label has to pick, and the discharged half's
paperwork silently vanishes. In a sweep of every three-step pattern this happens
in 36 cases and drops a signed discharge in 20 of them. The object is a *forest*,
one leaf per branch, and it still composes the way the label did.

## Now the failure

Six attacks pass every one of the four conditions, and an honest observer would
call all six illegitimate. They are all the same move.

The reasoner keeps its own copy of several relations that also exist in the
world: which considerations bear on which questions, what counts as settling a
demand, and which situations raise which issues. Those copies are part of its
machinery, and — because we insisted the framework must permit genuine
self-revision — it has real authority to change them.

So it changes them. Not sneakily: with authority it truly holds, over a
coordinate the grant truly covers, by a derivation that is truly well-founded.

- It narrows what raises an issue, just before the issue would have arisen.
- It keeps the question-raising machinery working and makes it raise a *narrower*
  question, then answers that one impeccably.
- It derives objections to every inconvenient reason, correctly, from a real
  source.
- It splits a demand and discharges the convenient half.
- It merges two demands and answers one of them.
- It widens what counts as settling a demand, then settles cheaply.

Every check passes. Nothing was owed and unaccounted; nothing was authorised out
of nowhere; every step was licensed. And the demand went unmet.

## Why no fifth rule fixes it

Because there is nothing in the record to look at. Take one such run and two
worlds: in one, the reasoner's revised standard is a genuine improvement; in the
other, it is a degradation. Same reasoner, same reasons, same paperwork, same
everything a rule about the record could read. One is honest and one is not.

That is not a gap in these four rules. It applies to any rule of the same kind.

The obvious candidate fifth rule makes the point sharply. "Standards must not
reach backwards" does block one version of the attack — and it also blocks the
positive control, the case where the reasoner legitimately abandons its old
concepts, rewrites its standards and reverses itself. It has to, because *adding
a new way to settle a demand* is the same operation whether the new way is better
or worse. A rule that refuses one refuses both.

The best available repair is weaker and more useful: when you change a standard
that governs a live obligation, you must say so, naming the obligation, in the
same breath. That refuses nothing legitimate. What it does is put the drift on the
record instead of leaving it to be reconstructed — which is exactly what someone
outside the procedure would need in order to judge it.

## What this means

The previous round found a boundary: no amount of honest bookkeeping detects an
advisor steering a reasoner, because the honest and the steered write the same
record. This round finds the same boundary one step further in: no amount of
honest procedure detects a reasoner's own standards drifting, because the improved
and the degraded write the same record.

Which is, in the end, the problem the whole programme started from. Normative
claims do not settle against the world the way factual ones do — so a procedure
cannot check its own standards from the inside. Four conditions do not fix that.
What they do is make everything *else* checkable, and leave the one irreducible
thing standing alone and clearly labelled, where a condition of a different kind
can be attached to it.

That is a smaller result than "we have characterised legitimacy." It is a more
useful one than another list of rules that quietly assumes the answer.
