# What this round did

The line had a lot of machinery and one name for all of it. The question put to
this round was whether splitting it into three — the statics of what reasons
permit, the conditions a history has to meet, and the learning question on top —
buys better mathematics or only better filing.

Mostly better mathematics, and not in the way the split predicted.

## The good news came from the archive

The proposal was that a history is legitimate when each step respects the reasons
available at the time, and no obligation quietly disappears across the whole run.
The second half of that was supposed to be new work. It is not: the monograph
this line already treats as authoritative proves it, under the heading of
identity across conceptual change. Every obligation is in exactly one state at
every date. No unresolved burden disappears. If every step of a history is
accepted then the composition is accepted, and each obligation's route through
the whole history is the composite of its routes through the steps.

That last one is the end-to-end theorem the round was sent to look for. It was
filed as a result about migration, and nobody had noticed it answers a question
about trajectories. Reorganizing is what made it findable.

## The bad news came from trying to break it

Six attacks. One is defeated cleanly: you cannot lose an obligation by retiring
the words it was written in, because obligations are not identified by words.
One shows the framework is not too strict — a reasoner may rewrite its own
standards, change its concepts and reverse itself, and still pass.

Three get through, and they get through for one reason. Both conditions ask what
the reasoner did with the reasons on its record. Neither asks how anything got
onto the record. So: write yourself the permission you need, then use it. Produce
an objection to every reason you dislike. Or simply never let an inconvenient
question be asked. All three pass every check, and the third cannot be fixed by
any rule about the record, because the thing it exploits is not on it.

The sixth is the one that matters most, and it is inherited rather than
discovered. Somewhere else in this workspace there is a Lean proof that a
reasoner working honestly and a reasoner repeating whatever it is told can leave
*the same record*. Not a similar record — the same one. This round rebuilds that
pair so that both write full normative paperwork, and confirms it: identical
record, both certified legitimate, and one of them has been moved half the
distance across the interval by its advisor.

So the honest summary of what clean books are worth: they exclude every
manipulation that works through the paperwork, and they exclude nothing that
works through influence. That is a real contribution to the deference project
provided nobody claims it is more.

## And one result that makes a target worse

The plan for the learning half was to compare the reasoner against alternative
strategies that would themselves have been legitimate. This round works out
exactly which strategies those are, and finds that if the reasoner's situation
moves around at all, there is only one of them: do nothing. The comparison is
then empty by construction. On the four-option example the family has exactly one
member.

That does not kill the learning programme. It says the family has to be indexed
by the situation, and then certifying a member costs as much as the certification
machinery the whole exercise was trying to keep out of the learner. That is the
same wall an earlier round hit, restated in a form where you can see what it would
take to get past it.

## Should the reorganization be kept

Yes, with the equation corrected. Two conditions were proposed; four are needed,
and the two new ones are not variations on the first two. And the umbrella name
should go, because the three layers are separately open and a single word for
them was hiding that.
