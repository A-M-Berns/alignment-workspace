## The author's thesis (as stated 2026-09-13, quoted in full so it can't drift)

> I don't think corrigibility is as anti-natural as people commonly make it out to be. I think the arguments for anti-naturality have hidden or under-scrutinized assumptions, so I'd like to go through them and address them. I haven't done a thorough literature review myself yet, so I'm not confident of what all the arguments and frameworks are. I think the basic argument is the argument against utility change, eg the ghandi murder pill argument. "You will predictably like the result of a universe optimized by a different utility function less." U(argmax(U)) ≥ U(argmax(U')). But corrigibility is just the desire to learn, grow, get better. Is that so anti-natural? Any change of utility function can be represented as a change in belief in an expanded sigma-algebra in which we add events representing utility, and representing the various possible utility functions (so we've got beliefs about values, and we then make decisions by taking linear expectation over this uncertainty, or perhaps some other rule). So we can think about change of belief instead of change of utility. But it is easy to come up with situations where change of belief is fine and desirable. Bayes updates are one example. More generally, any accuracy-increasing modification. Just have to obey the reflection principle. But we can always re-represent radical probabilist updates as classic bayesian updates in an expanded sigma algebra. This is called superconditioning. Sam Eisenstat observed that we can choose the expanded sigma algebra to be meta-beliefs, ie beliefs about what you believe; more concretely, the observation event is always "you believe this now" (you observe your new beliefs). This is an embedded-agency, radical-probabilist version of "observations": you observe your own mental state, rather than some inputs (eg retinas) on an agent boundary. The environment pushes the mental state around however it does. That pushing around may or may not be legitimate, ie accuracy-inducing, ie fit with the reflection principle. An incorrigible agent is just one that doesn't believe it has anything to learn from other agents. So long as learning is anticipated to be legitimate, the reflection principle holds, so there is no fully updated deference problem: a corrigible agent already believes what it expects to be legitimately taught. Failures of corrigibility result from failures of legitimacy detection, so it is crucial that such an agent have quite accurate beliefs on the subject of legitimacy, particularly for highly capable agents. It seems like the agent naturally wishes to maintain the legitimacy of its modifications, eg help repair the shutdown button. This is just a consequence of Good's theorem; information is valuable, and legitimate modifications are just "information channels". The shutdown button is an information channel.

### Item 1 — the utility-change argument (opened and closed 2026-09-13)

- **AGREED.** Arguments by example (the murder pill, Omohundro's arsonist) establish resistance to value change only for the example, and risk over-weighting such examples. Running the value-change → belief-change transform makes this obvious, because everyone already knows that some belief changes are welcome.
- **AGREED (the author's framing, accepted by Claude).** The belief/value distinction is representational, not real: the same change in the agent can be written either way. Modelling a system as an expected-utility maximiser puts no physical constraint on it, so nothing forces a "fixed object". Under radical probabilism everything may change, including the prior over value hypotheses and even the sigma-algebra; the only prohibition is probability-zero events becoming positive, which non-dogmatism handles (only logical impossibilities get zero). Superconditioning re-represents any such change as Bayesian when that is convenient.
- **WITHDRAWN (Claude).** The "residue" claim of 2026-09-13 — that values-as-beliefs relocates a fixed object to the meta-level and that Omohundro's hardening argument re-applies there. The author's response re-applies at every level too: the hardening argument reasons from a typical case in which environmental modification is harmful noise rather than useful correction, and agents may or may not distrust specific sources of modification.
- **CLAUDE (refinement, not yet vetted).** What survives is relational, not an object: at each moment the agent's current state (its meta-beliefs about sources of modification, i.e. where reflection holds) is the arbiter of legitimacy for the next change, and that arbiter is itself revisable next round. "Resists changes its current state judges illegitimate" is just agency. So every substantive anti-naturality argument has to be a claim that the agent's current legitimacy judgment *will* reject the overseers' actual corrections — a typical-case claim about the world, to be assessed argument by argument (items 6, 7, 8), not a structural theorem.
- **OPEN (the author).** The real difficulty from this item is representational: it is not obvious how to represent a Bayesian agent thinking about all the possible ways it could be modified. Richness assumptions on the hypothesis space (in the spirit of non-dogmatism) are needed to exclude impoverished-space cases such as the opiates/stimulants oscillation in [soares-2015-corrigibility](CITATIONS.md#soares-2015-corrigibility) / [arbital-corrigibility](CITATIONS.md#arbital-corrigibility).
- **DEFERRED.** 1a: Claude's proposed amendment "incorrigible = legitimacy judgment rejects the correction, not = nothing to learn" (Taylor 2015's morality-sensor agent). Judged separate from item 1; raise when relevant, probably item 6.
- **1b RESOLVED (the author, 2026-09-13; Claude concurs).** "Tower or fixed rule?" was a false dichotomy. The legitimacy-judging layer is the same probability distribution that judges everything else: $P_t$'s conditionals on future-total-state events ("I will believe $Q$", $Q$ including its own meta-beliefs) are its legitimacy judgments, so one distribution handles every level at once and there is no infinite regress. Two clarifications (CLAUDE, offered): (i) the distribution must live on a self-referential space — beliefs about one's own future beliefs — so "no regress" becomes an existence/consistency requirement, which is the representational problem of item 1 in its exact form; reflective oracles (Fallenstein–Taylor–Christiano 2015, from memory, not in corpus) and logical induction are the known partial solutions. (ii) No regress also means no outside view: the judge of the $t\to t+1$ transition is $P_t$, the judge at $t+1$ is $P_{t+1}$, so an illegitimate transition that produces a self-endorsing successor is judged illegitimate only by the past self. The whole design load therefore sits on the *accuracy of $P_t$'s meta-conditionals about modification sources* — a first-order calibration requirement, not a regress — which is the author's "failures of legitimacy detection" and is what items 6–8 test. Precedent in the corpus: Armstrong's sequential unbiasedness in [armstrong-2016-double-indifference-is-better-indifference](CITATIONS.md#armstrong-2016-double-indifference-is-better-indifference) is exactly this one-condition reflection across the estimator sequence, and he shows it removes the estimator-manipulation incentive (shortens item 4).
