# Research Ideas May 2026

I'm writing down my current thoughts about research directions, in order to sharpen my plan for research over the summer.

The overall idea here is to try to improve our state of knowledge with respect to the question: _under what conditions can humans justifiably trust AI?_ I start by examining conditions under which trust between rational agents can be established (eg, between an agent and its future self), in the hopes of incrementally building closer to a realistic picture of human-AI trust.

This often requires clarifying our picture of single-agent rationality (eg, decision theory, epistemology).

## Unbounded Embedded Agency

Cole Wyeth's [Unbounded Embedded Agency](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/B6gumHyuxzR5yn5tH/unbounded-embedded-agency-aedt-w-r-t-rosi) seeks to solve all of the problems of Embedded Agency except those which result from boundedness (computability). I think it accomplishes worthwhile things, but I also think the setup has several issues and could be improved in a number of ways.

To briefly summarize, Cole proposes a variant of AIXI based on reflective oracles, which uses EDT to select actions individually (no planning ahead), and learns via the reflective-oracle Solomonoff distribution over the entire history (actions and observations). This means the agent treats the source of its own actions as just as mysterious as the observation bits; they're all the same. It learns its own decision procedure empirically, not by having special knowledge designed in.

### Improbable Actions

Cole notes that there are open problems regarding how such an agent reasons about low-probability actions. As this agent becomes confident of its own source code, it must place very low probability on actions which that source code won't select. When considering whether to take low-probability actions, its hypothetical reasoning must be based on increasingly strange suppositions, IE, contrived worlds where it turns out to be wrong about the nature of its source code in spite of all the evidence so far. Can this cause any problems? If so, can these problems be solved, eg with [Conditional Oracles](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Rcwv6SPsmhkgzfkDw/edt-solves-5-and-10-with-conditional-oracles)?

One problem with EDT is how it handles actions if those actions have probability zero. Daniel Herrmann suggests that we ignore such actions, accepting that they're out of reach. As discussed in Embedded Agency, agents who have this property and also the further property that they assign probability zero to proven results will in fact rule out some actions using short proofs (via Lob's theorem). These are informally known as "spurious proofs": they're sound proofs, but they seem to rule an action out with no "real" justification. Which actions get ruled out in this way depends on details of the proof search and tie-breaking behavior, not on how good those actions are, so such agents cannot reasonably be called rational (unless the proof search is best-first in a specific sense).

This problem can be solved with the so-called "chicken rule": if you prove you won't take an action, then take it! (This ends up being very similar to the best-first proof search I mentioned earlier, known as modal UDT.) Paired with an assumption that anything not proven false must have greater than zero probability, the chicken rule ensures that there will not be any zero-probability actions. However, Troll Bridge shows that this solution doesn't always give the right answer; you can't have a spurious proof in the original sense, but a similar Lob argument can show that we won't take an action unless we're inconsistent. Since inconsistency can have bad consequences, this can lead to us avoiding such an action (thus enabling the proof).

Cole's proposal avoids spurious proofs by virtue of not knowing its own source code. This seems like a weak defense. Like the chicken rule, it works by forcing action probabilities to be positive. Similarly, though, if the agent is mostly confident about its own source code, taking actions it has proven it won't take (proof assuming its source code) might have negative evidential consequences (alternative choices of source code which perform worse). Can this idea cause problems in (something resembling) Cole's framework? Do Cole's carefully constructed oracles avoid it?

### Interpretational Issues

Cole's main result shows that, with his agent setup, if you are $1-\delta$-confident about your decision procedure, then your actions will be within $\varepsilon$ of optimal. That is: for any level of approximate optimality you'd like to achieve, there is a level of self-knowledge which allows you to achieve it. Essentially, this is an optimality result in a setting where optimality cannot always be achieved; the agent doesn't know how its own decisions are made, so even while acting rationally, its overall strategy can be poor due to a lack of self-trust. Cole's theorem shows conditions where global optimality of strategy can still be achieved.

Initially, I thought that even though Cole's theorem is not a tiling theorem, it was a close relative. Cole's proof bridges the crucial step from syntactic knowledge of one's own algorithm ("I mechanically work like this") to optimality ("my strategy is high-expected-value"). This sounds very useful to a proof that an agent does not intentionally self-modify. The big problem is that Cole's supposition of $1-\delta$-confidence in your own decision procedure necessarily rules out self-modification. If you put Cole's agent into an environment where self-modification is rational, then the probability of "I mechanically work like this" (where "like this" is its initial source code) is going to be small!

This problem also muddies the interpretation of the theorem as an optimality result, not just its relevance to tiling. What looks like a self-knowledge assumption sneakily rules out some environments of interest. It would be good to address this somehow, EG by distinguishing between self-knowledge and environmental concerns, or by distinguishing self-modification from environment-forced modification. I'm not sure what fix would be best.

### Updatelessness

Relatedly, what is the relationship between this theorem and updateless decision theory? A naive reading of the theorem suggests that UDT is not needed for optimality. The notion of optimality is the same one which UDT cares about (expected value of the policy), particularly when applying the theorem to the empty history. Yet, the decision algorithm this optimality result is proven for is updateful! How does an updateful decision procedure obtain this kind of optimality? I think there are a few reasons:

- As already noted, the self-knowledge assumption sneakily rules out some environments. If an environment requires updateless reasoning to solve optimally, but it also allows the agent to self-modify into an updateless decision procedure, then it'll be ruled out by the self-knowledge assumption! This shows how bad the assumption is: in the context of self-modification, the self-knowledge assumption rules out environments that could allow us to notice the suboptimality of the decision procedure.
- Additionally, reflective oracles assume that oracle calls are independent of each other. If the environment contains a powerful predictor Omega who is modeled as making oracle calls, the agent's actual choices will not be modeled as correlated with those calls. Since the epistemic states considered are computationally omniscient, agents know the (stochastic) output of any decision procedure perfectly, so correlations between their decisions and Omega's prediction must result from uncertainty about decision procedures (taking specific actions provides evidence about which decision procedure is in use). Modeling Omega as emulating the decision procedure directly instead of using oracle calls doesn't change this (independent runs of the same code will, similarly, be uncorrelated in Reflective Oracles).
- Relatedly, although Cole doesn't spell out all the technical details, my understanding is that his notion of $\varepsilon$-optimal simulates the consequences of a policy by _interleaving conditional probabilities_; a stream of alternating observations and actions (a history) is generated by sampling observations from $\xi$ (the reflective-oracle Solomonoff distribution, rOSI), but sampling the actions from $\pi$. This way of thinking about the consequences of a policy doesn't let the environment depend on that policy in arbitrary ways; an action can only have an impact after it actually occurs. Thus, updateful behavior always looks fine. For example, this method evaluates consequences as if Omega cannot accurately predict what you'll do (even if _you do believe Omega can predict you!_). I think Cole's essay might be trying to justify this by describing the result as "self-reflective AIXI converges to AIXI" -- the idea isn't to show a broad optimality result, but more specifically to show optimality in the classic AIXI case where the agent is in fact "outside of the environment" like someone playing a video game.

Supposing we fix the issues with the self-knowledge assumption, I think the other two issues might be somewhat interchangeable. If the formalism were switched to a variant of reflective oracles which allowed correlation, then the definition of optimality might no longer rule out Omega predicting you. If the definition of optimality were fixed in some other way, then the reflective oracles in themselves might not be causing any problems. I'm not sure which angle is most promising.

I'm imagining a theorem something like: if a UDT1.0 agent $(1-\delta)$-believes that its policy is UDT1.1, then it is $\varepsilon$-optimal. UDT1.0 is the version of UDT that chooses single actions at a time, very much like Cole's decision procedure here (but updateless). UDT1.1 is the version of UDT that chooses its whole policy at once, meaning that (in the classic UDT setting, where the agent is confident of its own source code) it is trivially $\varepsilon$-optimal (it is just defined as the optimal policy). UDT1.0 is not necessarily optimal in this way, because it can be in a "bad equilibrium" with itself (failing a Stag Hunt); a bad strategy cannot always be improved incrementally by improving individual actions. Hence the idea behind the theorem: UDT1.0 _is_ optimal if it _believes_ its decisions are being made by UDT1.1.

I believe this also simplifies and improves the result in a different way: Cole had to construct a specific reflective oracle for his theorem, which is both an annoying technical aside and weakens the result (the result isn't true for arbitrary reflective oracles). I think belief that you're UDT1.1 does the work that Cole's constructed oracle does, since (if I understand correctly) the role of his special oracle is also to avoid self-coordination problems.

That's just my current idea about how to stick close to Cole's result while improving it along the lines I see fit; I haven't really finished thinking through all the issues I've brought up. No doubt there are many interesting questions to investigate, including notions of optimality, tiling (reflective consistency, self-modification), learning, and further investigation of embeddedness issues.

The impact of this project would be to refine our picture of computationally unbounded intelligence, with the hope that doing so will also help our understanding of bounded intelligence, and AI safety.

### Will this help our understanding of bounded intelligence?

Writing my last two papers, I've tried to stick faithfully to Yudkowsky's program for decision theory; in particular, two assumptions:

* Reflective consistency is a very powerful and productive constraint to explore in decision theories; we should be investigating decision theories which would choose themselves instrumentally.
* In order to characterize reflective consistency in a real-life-applicable way, it is crucial that it be based on "Vingean" reasoning; that is: we must trust decision procedures to perform well on general principles, not by virtue of being able to predict them precisely.

I still believe strongly in the importance of reflective consistency, but the Vingean principle rules out models such as those based on reflective oracles, where beliefs are computationally omniscient. I think computationally omniscient models can often be a good analogy for more bounded models, as reflective oracles are for logical induction. It is important, in research, to focus on the simplest case where it is possible to study something you don't yet understand. Cole's work helped convince me that there's more to be understood about non-computable models of intelligence. My work prior to that has led me to believe that the computable case is very challenging. Thus, it makes sense to try and further refine our understanding of the computationally omniscient case.

Why would such results be of interest, if they break down under perfect knowledge of one's own source code? The idea that decision theories should still work even with certain knowledge of their own source code is similar to the Vingean constraint on reflective consistency, in that it helps force one to consider a difficult and confusing case. Again, my hope is that the computationally omniscient case can help by way of providing good analogies. In Cole's framework, source code must remain unknown, but the behavior of any given code is always known. These two can substitute for each other, to some extent; not knowing the source code is somewhat like not knowing code's behavior. Where the analogy seems strained (eg, oracle calls are always independent), we can seek to improve it.

## What is a decision problem?

I recently spent some time [thinking about the definition of a decision problem](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/CDkbYSFTwggGE8mWp/coherent-care), inspired by Nate Soare's remarks in [Decisions are for making bad outcomes inconsistent](https://intelligence.org/2017/04/07/decisions-are-for-making-bad-outcomes-inconsistent/). The aim is to make "decision theory is systematized winning" or "why ain'cha rich?" arguments very clear, in a way that avoids the common response "rich by whose measure?" (that is: the definition of optimality is part of the subject matter of decision theories, so it cannot be used to judge between them).

The formalism and arguments could use some work. In particular, I gave the type of a decision procedure as $(S,A) \to A$. The two $A$s are not really the same; the first one is a set of actions, while the second one is an individual action from that set. (This should have been formalized with a dependent type.)

It could also be interesting to relate this sort of optimality notion to tiling, the CDT vs EDT debate, and other interesting questions. The main reason I include it in this list is because I think the ideas could be related to other projects listed here; I don't have some specific project for the summer in this section, but I wanted to include it nonetheless.

## Deference Done Better, Done Better

Recently I've been interested in the model of alignment which emerges from [Deference Done Better](https://philpapers.org/archive/DORDDB.pdf) by Dorst, Levinstein, Salow, Husic and Fitelson; [A Decision-Theoretic Approach for Managing Misalignment](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.15584) by Herrmann, Chari, Qian, Sharvesh, and Levinstein; and [Margins of Misalignment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AA5nRwChWbY), unpublished work by Herrmann and Levinstein.

Dorst et al show that, in a specific sense (and under specific background assumptions, of course) _epistemic trust implies instrumental trust_. More specifically, there is a well-known principle called **reflection** which is due to Bas van Fraassen, [Belief and the Will](https://joelvelasco.net/teaching/3865/van%20fraassen%2084%20-%20belief%20and%20the%20will.pdf). Stating it a bit informally:

**Reflection:** The reflection principle holds between one perspective $A$ and another $B$ just in case $\forall_X P_A \big(X | P_B (X) = y \big) = y$, often rewritten as $\forall_X P_A \big(X | P_B (X) > y \big) > y$ or other variations to avoid problems such as conditioning on probability-zero events.

_Roughly,_ this principle says that if $A$ came to know $B$'s beliefs, $A$ would simply adopt those beliefs. This gloss is dishonest because it confuses the conditional probability $P_I(Q|R)$ for "what $I$ would believe if $I$ learned $R$" -- that is, it assumes updates are Bayesian updates. This is a common assumption, but it can be relaxed without creating dutch-bookability (see [Radical Probabilism](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/xJyY5QkQvNJpZLJRo/radical-probabilism-1)). Indeed, one way of understanding Reflection is that it gives the weaker rationality principle for updates: $P_A$ can validly update to $P_B$ so long as Reflection is obeyed. (However, note that the reflection principle does not actually constrain $P_B$ at all, only $P_A$'s opinion about $P_B$.)

I find the term "reflection" to be a near-meaningless name for this property, and much over-used for other unrelated ideas besides. I personally prefer to say "$A$ **endorses** $B$" to describe this state of affairs, instead. (I suppose "reflection" is intended to bring "reflective equilibrium" to mind -- reflection is like a single step towards reflective equilibrium. I could tolerate "$A$ reflectively endorses $B$" but just "endorses" seems a bit better.)

Amongst other results (and simplifying for the sake of brevity), Deference Done Better and Managing Misalignment show that **endorsement $\iff$ deference**; that is, humans should happily trust AI to make decisions on their behalf precisely when humans endorse AI beliefs.

### Updatelessness

The notion of alignment as endorsement appears to be an updateful one; endorsement and deference come apart when actions can have acausal consequences, since a more-knowledgeable future self might refuse a profitable counterfactual mugging, get the wrong answer in a transparent newcomb, etc.

In a more updateless context, are there any substantial ideas which characterize deference? Endorsement isn't sufficient, or necessary. However, it isn't the case that UDT only endorses its own exact prior, either. There are real improvements which UDT will accept. Can we study what sort of "update" UDT will happily defer to? Can we characterize it by an endorsement-like concept? (Perhaps some generalization of endorsement?) Ideas from [UDT1.01](https://www.lesswrong.com/s/CCtgwXPXWxSiFWFnf) might be relevant.

This would constitute significant progress on [open-minded updatelessness](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/open-minded-updatelessness). My loose conjecture, based on my [Geometric UDT](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Gmaq7vMj9NWLyQkEx/geometric-udt), is that we should be more updateful about "moral uncertainty" (EG, an aligned AI should be mostly/completely updateful about human values), while remaining updateless about other matters.

I don't think we _need_ to add updatelessness to the formalism; there are many important questions we can ask without doing so, and I expect updatelessness to be quite challenging to integrate. Endorsement naturally lives in a Radical Probabilist picture, where updates are not indexed by evidence. However, superconditioning can bridge that gap, and I do expect that there would be many interesting things to say.

### Representational Issues

Probability frames treat the principal's beliefs as given, and the agent's beliefs as uncertain. The probability space is assumed to be finite, meaning the agent's beliefs have been narrowed down to a finite set of possibilities. One might prefer to model the set of possible beliefs as countable or uncountable. I suppose the ideas probably generalize without too much trouble? I worry a bit that there is a "size problem" being hidden here; the principal and the agent are being modeled in an asymmetric way. Is the model missing something important about them recursively modeling each other?

The model _certainly_ doesn't account for the principal or agent recursively modeling _themselves_; EG, it doesn't deal with paradoxical sentences such as the self-referential sentence $G$: "my credence in $G$ is less than $1\over 2$". Is this important to the phenomena studied by the paper?  [Dorst thinks our uncertainty about our beliefs changes the picture of rationality significantly](https://www.kevindorst.com/research.html), impacting the theory of common knowledge, Aumann's agreement theorem, and other important ideas.

Deference Done Better and Managing Misalignment use the common trick of representing beliefs-about-beliefs via random variables: you've got a set of worlds, $\omega \in \Omega$, and an agent's beliefs at a set time are represented as a function of this. A statement like "$P_A(X)<{1\over 2}$" is represented as an event by taking the set of worlds where it is true. This model is consistent with full self-knowledge.

In contrast, Gödel's Diagonal Lemma shows that under modest conditions, if a language can represent a predicate (such as "$P_A (\_) < {1\over 2}$") then it can represent a self-referential sentence which asserts that predicate of itself. Such sentences make perfect self-knowledge inconsistent.

The random-variable way of doing things avoids these problems and makes for a simpler mathematical picture, but it makes the gamble that Gödel-type issues of self-reference are not relevant to the phenomena of interest. This seems like a misstep in the study of common knowledge especially. The trick essentially allows the theory of probabilistic reasoning to neglect logical concerns.

I do in fact think that the random variable trick allows the authors to study important aspects of alignment while avoiding annoying technical complications. Still, it could be useful to refine the picture further by including the Diagonal Lemma in our analysis. Such an analysis seems to argue that perfect alignment is, in a certain sense, impossible. However, I expect there will be an appropriate weakening of the results which preserves their essential nature.

The [Logical Induction paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.03543) argues that the full reflection principle is actually undesirable due to self-referential sentences, and articulates a weaker desirable principle which holds for Logical Induction (Section 4.12: Self-Trust). It would be good to check whether this weaker notion of endorsement could imply a weaker notion of deference.

It would also be possible to study these phenomena in [Paul Christiano's self-referential probability framework](https://intelligence.org/files/DefinabilityTruthDraft.pdf).

### Fast Student, Slow Teacher (Merging Logical Inductors)

One big problem with Managing Misalignment is that the approach is not constructive at all: it characterizes alignment, but doesn't give any advice about how to build an aligned AI.

A natural idea is to switch to Logical Induction (aka Garrabrant Induction) as the formalism; this comes with a computable algorithm. Logical Induction will come to approximately endorse itself in a reflection-like sense, which seems like a good start. However, naively, this only gives us a picture of (boundedly) rational agents who come to trust _themselves_. AI safety requires a picture where _humans can justifiably come to trust AI_. This suggests we study conditions under which one logical inductor will trust another.

I believe one logical inductor will come to trust another on subsequences with good feedback (given some other assumptions about the inductors' knowledge about each other). However, humans need to be able to trust AI in circumstances that do not involve good feedback: many things we wish to trust AI about are not directly observable (EG human flourishing, human values, philosophical questions, ethics/morality, emotions, scientific questions which must be approached with the scientific method, etc). How can we achieve trust on such questions?

One idea (due to Sam Eisenstat) starts with a slow but trusted inductor (representing humans) and a fast but untrusted inductor (representing potentially-misaligned AI). From these ingredients, is there a way we can build a new logical inductor combining the strengths of both (fast and aligned)? I'll write $H_t$ to represent the human beliefs at time $t$, and $A_t$ to represent the AI beliefs at time $t$. We can construct a new sequence of beliefs $B_t$ by asking the AI what it expects the human beliefs to be at time $f(t)$. Sam's conjecture: if the AI has good feedback about the human beliefs, and if $f$ grows fast enough, then $B_t$ will itself be a logical inductor, and furthermore, its beliefs will be endorsed by $H_t$ (in the weak sense that makes sense in a logical induction context).

If that all works out, I think the natural next idea to explore is: can we get 'good feedback' from other assumptions, instead of assuming it? Humans are not always good at introspection; it may be unwise to suppose that humans can report $H_t$ faithfully. Instead, I think the natural "basin of corrigibility" style argument goes more like: the AI starts out with a "good enough" understanding of human-endorsed reasoning; the AI aims this at the problem of figuring out humans better; this produces a more refined estimate of human-endorsed reasoning; this is again pointed at the problem of understanding humans better; etc. "Good feedback" is a consequence of facts about the AI, facts about the humans, and facts about the interaction between them. This seems quite challenging to formalize in an appropriate way.

All of this ignores inner-alignment concerns. A trader inside the AI can wait until humans trust the AI, and then do something untrustworthy. Such traders will be eliminated by learning eventually, supposing that humans can survive such treacherous turns with the whole setup intact. Such problems might be ruled out by sufficiently extreme "the AI starts out close enough to human reasoning" style assumptions, but a more realistic safety story would probably add other ideas such as interpretability or cautiousness.

## Modeling Legitimacy & Corrigibility

If Alice endorses Bob's beliefs, this is not really a property of Bob's beliefs, but rather Alice's. Roughly, Alice thinks Bob's beliefs are correlated with reality. Alice should endorse Bob's beliefs if the belief-formation process was truth-tracking, and not otherwise. Alice might endorse Bob because she thinks Bob is studious, scientific, logical, etc. Alice might not endorse Bob because she thinks Bob is drunk, doesn't care about the subject matter, etc.

My word for this phenomenon is **legitimacy**. A belief-formation process is legitimate if we should endorse its results. (I say "should" because legitimacy is something we can be uncertain about; we can fail to endorse someone, not realizing that their reasoning is legitimate.)

An appropriate formal model of legitimacy seems important for modeling AI alignment. Reasoning correctly about legitimacy is relevant to wireheading, reward hacking, and human manipulation: such things are bad because they compromise the legitimacy of the training process. AI should avoid such things in the same way that most humans avoid highly addictive drugs: they don't try such drugs in the first place because they anticipate getting addicted, and don't currently evaluate that outcome favorably, even though they know they would enjoy it if it happened. (They don't endorse a potential future addiction-based opinion.)

I believe a good model of legitimacy could also shed light on corrigibility: AI can be corrigible in cases where it sees human shutdown/modification decisions as legitimate. Formalizing this notion of corrigibility and proving some theorems about what it can and cannot accomplish seems high-priority.

## Agency & Abstraction

The research direction I described in [Research Reflections](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/4gosqCbFhtLGPojMX/research-reflections). The goal is to combine a picture of agency with a picture of abstraction, to get a picture of the intentional stance; that is, to characterize when it is rational to regard some observed phenomena as agentic.

My main idea here is to start with Vingean agency, by which I mean the sort of epistemic position we have when watching someone who is more skilled than us at chess: we cannot predict exactly what they will do, but we can predict that they will win. Can such a thing be characterized well by a theory of abstraction?
