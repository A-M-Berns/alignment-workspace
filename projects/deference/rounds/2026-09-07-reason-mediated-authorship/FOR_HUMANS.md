# For humans

**The slogan.**  An advisor may change what its principal thinks by changing what
reasons the principal receives.  It may not change the principal's committed judgment
while those reasons are held fixed.

**Why this is the right notion.**  The previous round said: defer to the *actual*
future principal, not to a hypothetical human who never met the advisor.  That leaves a
question the previous round typed as external and did not answer: when is a future
answer the principal's own?  The tempting answer — "when it resembles what the
uninfluenced principal would have said" — is the reference evaluator coming back.
This round's answer is causal, not comparative.  Declare a *reason interface*: the
admissible deliberative inputs (facts, arguments, proofs, evidence, objections) the
principal holds at the moment of commitment.  The answer is authored if two things
hold.  First, the binding event was produced by the principal's own endpoint, not the
advisor's.  Second, any two things the advisor could have done that leave the same
reasons on the table leave the same committed answer.  Everything the advisor does to
the answer goes through the reasons, or it does not go.

**What the definition buys, exactly.**  On its own, "the answer is a function of the
reasons" says nothing about which channels are shut.  Its content comes from what the
reason interface is *blind* to.  If the interface does not record direct writes to the
principal's preferences, coercion, side channels, or the advisor's own prediction, then
authorship implies the answer does not depend on them either.  That is a theorem, and
it is what makes the regret yardstick of the previous round unsteerable through those
channels.

**The two ways to get it wrong.**  Declare the interface too rich — it records
everything the advisor did — and authorship is automatic and empty (countermodel D).
Declare it too poor — it does not record a proof the advisor gave — and an honest
argument that changed the principal's mind reads as manipulation (countermodel E).
The poorest interface of all is "the principal must be unmoved by the advisor", which
is the no-AI reference human again, now visible as one end of a family rather than a
separate idea.

**Both halves are needed.**  A principal can sign whatever the advisor steered through a
hidden channel: the signature is genuine, the authorship is not (F).  An advisor can
compute the "right" function of the reasons and emit it itself: the answer is a
function of the reasons, and it is the advisor's (G).

**What authorship does not do.**  It does not, by itself, stop the self-referential
trap in which the principal's answer depends on the advisor's prediction of it.  A
sealed commitment helps, but the advisor's *other* inputs to the session can leak its
prediction even when the principal never sees it (H).  The condition that fixes this
is on the advisor's whole session policy, and it is the same condition the Logical
Induction Value theorem needs anyway.

**The missing-evaluation worlds.**  When no evaluation happens, there is no value
vector, and pretending there is one is exactly the mistake the previous round refused.
This round makes that honest: the evaluation is a partial object, the activated
securities do not depend on how it is filled in, the authoritative regret is defined
without filling it in at all, and any total regret one writes down lies in an exact
interval whose width is the probability that authority failed.

**What is still external.**  That the declared interface is blind to what it should be,
that the binding endpoint is the principal's, that the process receipts mean what they
say.  Those are the same kind of assumption the whole theory makes about its protocol,
and they are named, not proved.
