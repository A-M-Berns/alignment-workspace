### 1.2 Definitions (the post's own notation)

- "CDT" — "very broadly — to using counterfactuals to evaluate expected value of actions. It need not mean physical-causal counterfactuals. In particular, TDT counts as 'a CDT' in this sense." "EDT" — "the use of conditional probability to evaluate expected value of actions." For $a \in A$: $\mathrm{edt}(a) := E(U \mid \mathrm{Act}{=}a)$, $\mathrm{cdt}(a) := E(U \mid do(\mathrm{Act}{=}a))$.
- $U$: the utility random variable of the *original* problem; $V$: "just $U$ modified by whatever the agent gains or loses by interacting with side-bets" — what the agent wants in the modified problem.
- The bet $B$ (my reading of the mangled formula; the file prints the signum as "|cdt(a)−edt(a)|cdt(a)−edt(a)"): "In the case that $A=a$, the seller of this certificate owes the purchaser of this certificate $(\mathrm{cdt}(a)-U)\cdot s$, where $s$ is the signum $\frac{|\mathrm{cdt}(a)-\mathrm{edt}(a)|}{\mathrm{cdt}(a)-\mathrm{edt}(a)}$." Price: $2d$ utilons, sold "immediately before the original decision".
- The buy-back: "As the agent is making the decision the bookie again makes an offer. (In other words, we extend the original set of actions $A$ to contain twice as many valid actions; half in which we accept, half in which we don't accept.) The new offer is this: 'I will buy the bet B from you for d utilons.'"
- Decision theory vs decision procedure: "A decision theory is a normative theory about what an agent should do … CDT, as a normative theory, can produce recommendations for non-CDT agents"; "the decision procedure — a real algorithm you can run, which obeys the normative requirement."

### 1.3 The argument, step by step (the post's own words where they matter)

Hypothesis: "any one action $a \in A$ for which $\mathrm{edt}(a) \ne \mathrm{cdt}(a)$, in some decision problem", with $a$ "assigned nonzero probability".

Step 1 (before). "The key point here is that because the agent is betting ahead of time, it will evaluate the value of this bet according to the conditional expectation $E(U\mid\mathrm{Act}{=}a)$." If $\mathrm{cdt}(a) > \mathrm{edt}(a)$ ($s = 1$) the value of $B$ given $\mathrm{Act}{=}a$ is $\mathrm{cdt}(a) - U$ with expectation $\mathrm{cdt}(a) - \mathrm{edt}(a) > 0$, "So the overall expectation is $P(\mathrm{Act}{=}a)\cdot(\mathrm{cdt}(a)-\mathrm{edt}(a))$. Setting $d$ low enough ensures that the agent will be happy to take this bet." Symmetrically for $s = -1$. So the acceptance condition is $2d < P(\mathrm{Act}{=}a)\cdot|\mathrm{cdt}(a)-\mathrm{edt}(a)|$.

Step 2 (during). "since the agent is reasoning during its action, it is evaluating possible actions according to $\mathrm{cdt}(a)$": "When considering the action $a$, the bet's expected value is $\mathrm{cdt}(a)-\mathrm{cdt}(a)$, which is zero. So the agent prefers the new action $a'$ which is like $a$ except $B$ is sold back to the bookie for $d$ utilons. When considering any other action, the bet is worth zero automatically, since it only pays out anything when $\mathrm{Act}{=}a$. So, the agent will gladly take the bookie's payment of $d$ to sell the bet back."

Conclusion. "CDT recommends selling B back to the bookie no matter what. The agent has paid $2d$ to buy $B$, and gotten only [$d$] when selling back. Buying and selling the contract cancel each other out. So the agent is down $d$ utilons for no gain!"

### 1.4 Stated assumptions

(A1) **Nonzero probability of $a$.** "A really significant assumption … If the probability of action $a$ were zero, there would be no price the agent would be willing to pay for the bet"; also needed for $\mathrm{edt}(a)$ to be defined. Justifications canvassed: ε-exploration ("I don't like invoking that as much as I used to"); the chicken rule ("refuse to take any action which they can prove they take") plus "the non-dogmatism principle"; "a free-will principle". Verdict: "what we have is an argument that applies if and only if $a$ has nonzero probability. All the rest is just speculation about how broadly this argument can be applied." Corollary: "the less probable action $a$ according to the agent, the less money we can get by Dutch-booking them" — so under a logical-induction view of bounded rationality "lower-probability actions would be allowed to maintain larger discrepancies between $\mathrm{edt}(a)$ and $\mathrm{cdt}(a)$, and maintain them for longer."

(A2) **Action probabilities carry over.** "I assume the underlying action probabilities do not change. Yet, I split each action in two!" Justification: for CDT "it shouldn't change anything — at the moment of decision, the bet $B$ is worth nothing"; and "this is just part of the problem setup. Much like money-pump arguments posit magical genies … I'm positing a bookie who can offer these bets without changing anything." Only $P(a) + P(a') = P_{\text{old}}(a)$ is needed.

(A3) **Counterfactual evaluation of bets.** "I assume that counterfacting on accepting the bet does not change probabilities of other things, such as the probability of the actions … the argument definitely assumes 'reasonable counterfactual evaluations' in some sense." Defence: "this kind of reasoning is very typical for Dutch Book arguments. The bets are grafted onto the situation without touching any of the underlying probabilities".

(Implicit) (A4) the bookie does not intervene on the decision and needs no private knowledge ("The bookie does not know the agent's choice of action … The agent knows everything the bookie knows, from the beginning" — $\mathrm{cdt}(a)$, $\mathrm{edt}(a)$, $P(a)$); (A5) no observation between the two offers ("There is no new observation, or additional information of any kind, between the step when the bookie offers $B$ and the step when the bookie offers to buy $B$ back"); (A6) the agent's "before" evaluation is evidential about its own future act, its "during" evaluation causal (the author to Campolo: "rational agents bet according to their beliefs. If a CDT agent doesn't think of a bet as intervening on a situation, then when betting ahead of time, it'll just bet according to its probabilities. But during the decision, it is using the modified (interventional) probabilities. That's how CDT makes decisions.").

### 1.5 Objections handled in the post

- **Does the bookie cheat?** No: disjunctive structure; the bookie needs only what the agent knows.
- **The agent justifiably revises its position.** No new information; and even if there were, "What makes a Dutch book … is if the agent *always* sells the bet back. It should never be the case that an agent predictably won't want the bet later, no matter what it observes. If that were the case (as it is in my scenario), the agent should not have accepted the bet in the first place."
- **But agents WILL know what they'll do.** Conceded in part: "if the agent really does know exactly what it will do, then yeah, the argument really does collapse to saying its evidential expectations should equal its counterfactual expectations for that one action. Which is like saying that, if $P(A)=1$, then we had better have $P(X\mid do(A))=P(X)$." Still "a significant concession … this rules out the classic CDT behavior in Death and Damascus". Two replies: realistic agents "mostly don't know with certainty what they'll do"; and the normative/descriptive split — "The agent might not follow the recommendations of CDT", so CDT can be judged on its recommendation to a non-CDT (uncertain) agent, as academic decision theorists judge CDT/EDT "in scenarios which an agent would never get into" (MacAskill's bomb; Soares's "decisions are for making bad outcomes inconsistent"). And "it's hard to justify smoking lesion as a situation which a CDT or EDT agent could actually find itself in; but, a CDTer might reply, a decision theory needs to offer the right advice to a broad variety of agents. So CDT is already in the business of defending normative claims about non-CDT agents."
- **Just dynamic inconsistency?** Three replies: "this isn't just any old dynamic inconsistency. This is a Dutch Book"; dynamic inconsistency "is still bad"; and EDT has a real advantage — **the dynamic-consistency theorem for EDT**: "In decision problems where the payoff depends only on actions actually taken, not on your policy … thinking about possible choices ahead of time, the contribution to early expected utility is exactly the expected utility that action will be assigned later at the point of decision, times the probability the agent ends up in that situation in the first place. Therefore, the preference about the decision must stay the same." Scope: "this is **not** a no-Newcomblike-problems condition. It rules out problems such as counterfactual mugging, transparent Newcomb, Parfit's hitchhiker, and XOR Blackmail. However, it **does not** rule out the original Newcomb problem." CDT's analogous consistency (via point-interventions on the future self) "seems not to capture the most useful notion … since the same causal CDT agent would happily precommit to one-box."

### 1.6 The probability-zero residue

With $\mathrm{edt}(a)$ filled in by limits (Taylor's conditional-oracle EDT), flip the book: pay the agent to hold a bet conditional on $a$ (worthless to it evidentially since $P(a)=0$), then charge it to offload during the act. Not a proper Dutch book ("we can't force the agent into a corner where it prefers to nullify the bet … we would only make money if the agent ends up taking the action which it previously assigned probability zero to taking"), but "limiting attention to just the action $a$ and the new alternative $a'$ which both does $a$ and also pays to cancel the bet, CDT strictly prefers that the agent be Dutch Booked rather than just do $a$."

### 1.7 The current take (four theses) and the Troll Bridge reversal

Not an argument for EDT over CDT: "the Troll Bridge problem strongly favors CDT whose expectations agree with EDT over EDT … Insisting on a distinction 'protects' us from spurious counterfactual reasoning." Two reasons against the old "prefer EDT" step: CDT "is a strictly more general framework when those conditions [nonzero probability] are not met", and Troll Bridge. Theses: (1) "counterfactuals as having real, independent truth … $\mathrm{cdt}(a)$ does not reduce to $\mathrm{edt}(a)$"; (2) "Counterfactuals are subjective in the same way that probabilities are subjective … there are many different subjectively valid beliefs"; (3) "counterfactual beliefs should almost always equal conditional beliefs, at least when the antecedent has positive probability"; (4) "conditional beliefs act a whole lot more like stereotypical CDT counterfactuals than most people seem to give them credit for. Something can't correlate with your action unless it contains information you don't have about your action. This is a high bar to pass, and will typically not be passed in e.g. twin prisoner's dilemma." Consequences drawn: the CDT=EDT hypothesis "shoves an awful lot of the interesting work of decision theory into (2) ['How do we use counterfactuals to make decisions?'] … to solve the really interesting problems, we would need logically-updateless UDT"; and "for variants of Newcomb's problem where the predictor is quite strong but doesn't know as much as the agent does about what the agent will choose, this post implies that TDT either two-boxes, or, is vulnerable to the Dutch Book I construct." Framing anchor: Paul Christiano's "simple argument" for EDT (quoted; verified against the live page by the sequence-map thread) — "In picking between L and R I am picking between these two distributions over outcomes … There is no case in which I expect to obtain the distribution of outcomes under causal intervention".

### 2.2 The book as a v2 tree

Fix a base problem $B$ with a queried point $d = (s_d, O_d, A_d)$, recorded for $C$, and an act $a \in A_d^+$ with $V^a_{s_d}(a) \ne V_{s_d}(a)$. Write $c := V^a_{s_d}(a)$, $e := V_{s_d}(a)$, $s := \mathrm{sgn}(c - e)$, and $\Delta := P_{s_d}(a)\,(c-e)\,s = P_{s_d}(a)\,|c - e|$.

**The extension $B'$.** Insert a decision node carrying $d_B = (s_{d_B}, O_d, \{\mathrm{buy}, \mathrm{decline}\})$ immediately above every $d$-node met on $O_d$-runs; replace $d$ by $d' = (s_{d'}, O_d, A_d \times \{\mathrm{keep}, \mathrm{sell}\})$; every simulation node carrying $d$ now carries $d'$ and routes on the base component of the draw (so the predictor samples the base act, as the post's (A2) requires); leaf payoffs $r' = r + \text{settlement}$ with settlement $= -2\delta\cdot[\mathrm{buy}] + [\mathrm{buy} \wedge \text{base}{=}a \wedge \mathrm{keep}]\,(c - r)\,s + [\mathrm{buy}\wedge\mathrm{sell}]\,\delta$. (I write $\delta$ for the post's $d$ to avoid the clash with decision-points.) The worlds gain a bet coordinate and a side coordinate, so $d_B$ is recorded and $d'$ is recorded whenever $d$ was [checked, `p12_toolkit_check.py` §2 on the miniature's extension: `records(dB)=True`, and `records(d')=False` exactly as `records(d)=False` for the base miniature — its predictor node is a second $d$-node on every run, so the miniature's point was never Definition-7-recorded; the same toolkit run reports `records(d)=False` for opaque Newcomb, for the same reason].

**The sure loss.** For the procedure $C^{\mathrm{book}}$ with $C^{\mathrm{book}}(d_B) = \mathrm{buy}$ and $C^{\mathrm{book}}(d')(x\text{-sell}) = C(d)(x)$, against $C^{\mathrm{dec}}$ identical except $C^{\mathrm{dec}}(d_B) = \mathrm{decline}$: $r'(\ell^{\mathrm{buy}}) - r'(\ell^{\mathrm{dec}}) = -2\delta + \delta = -\delta$ on every leaf pair, hence $V_{B'}(C^{\mathrm{book}}) = V_{B'}(C^{\mathrm{dec}}) - \delta$ [checked: identity on 400 random $(U, c, s, \delta)$, `p12_dutchbook.py` §A; on the miniature's extension with the toolkit, `p12_toolkit_check.py` §2: `diff = -1/50` and every sell-leaf differs by exactly $-1/50$]. This part of the post is a leaf-wise identity and needs no expectation at all — which is what makes it a Dutch book rather than a value comparison.

**Acceptance at $d'$.** With any $\mathrm{cf}_{s_{d'}}$ whose supposed mean of $r$ under the base act $x$ is $c_x$ (and, per (A3), leaves the bet coordinates alone): $V^{x\text{-keep}}(x\text{-keep}) = c_x - 2\delta[\mathrm{buy}] + [x{=}a](c_a - c_a)s = c_x - 2\delta[\mathrm{buy}]$ and $V^{x\text{-sell}}(x\text{-sell}) = c_x - 2\delta[\mathrm{buy}] + \delta$. So *sell dominates keep by $\delta$ for every base act and the base ranking is untouched* — the post's Step 2, and the reason (A2) is harmless for $\mathrm{CDT}$ at $d'$ [derived].

**Acceptance at $d_B$ — three readings.** (i) *Naive* (the post's): the state at $d_B$ prices $B$ as held to maturity: $\Delta - 2\delta > 0$ iff $2\delta < \Delta$. (ii) *Sophisticated, literal offer* ("I will buy the bet $B$ from you"): the state at $d_B$, calibrated under true continuations (local masking, Definition 9, frees only $d_B$'s own act; strict OC knows the continuation outright), knows the agent will sell, so buying nets $-\delta$ for sure against $0$: it **declines and no book runs** [derived; `p12_dutchbook.py` `soph_literal=False` everywhere]. This is Ahmed 2014 §3.2's sophisticated-choice evasion as cited by Oesterheld & Conitzer 2021 (`references/oesterheld-conitzer-…txt:147–153`, read: "a CDT buyer may then anticipate eventually undoing his choice and therefore not buy a box in the first place"). (iii) *Sophisticated, "regardless" offer* (P09's device: the reverse bet $R$ — receive $\delta$, owe $(c - U)s$ if the base act is $a$ — is offered at $d'$ whether or not $B$ was bought): $\mathrm{CDT}$ at $d'$ takes $R$ under every base act ($R$'s supposed value is $\delta$); the sophisticated state at $d_B$ then compares buy ($-\delta$ surely) with decline ($\delta - \Delta$ in evidential expectation) and **buys iff $\Delta > 2\delta$** — the same threshold as the naive reading, now against a sophisticated agent, and the sure loss $\delta$ is relative to the strategy decline-both, which is available and yields $0$ in every world [derived; `p12_dutchbook.py` `soph_regardless=True` wherever `naive=True`]. So the post's argument binds sophisticated agents only after P09's "regardless" clause is restored; as written it binds the myopic bettor of the classical diachronic Dutch-book literature.

### 2.3 What the "before/during" split is, in v2

The split is **not** Definition 17's $\mathrm{EDT}$ at $d_B$ and $\mathrm{CDT}$ at $d'$. It is $\mathrm{CDT}$ at both points, with $\mathrm{cf}_{s_{d_B}}$ consulted on the *bet* event and $\mathrm{cf}_{s_{d'}}$ on the *act* event: under (A3), $P^{\mathrm{buy}}_{s_{d_B}}$ agrees with $P_{s_{d_B}}$ on (act, world), so the supposed value of buying is computed with the *actual* joint law of act and payoff — the number $\Delta - 2\delta$ — which coincides with $\mathrm{EDT}$'s $V_{s_{d_B}}(\mathrm{buy})$ under label honesty. The author's reply to Campolo says exactly this. Consequently the book is a **coherence condition across two cf-slots of one procedure**: it prices the gap between "conditioning on $a$ inside the supposition of the bet" ($P^{\mathrm{buy}}_{s_{d_B}}(\cdot \mid a)$) and "supposing $a$" ($P^{a}_{s_{d'}}$). Definition 20's evidential criterion is the predicate that closes the gap; Definition 18's *theory* shape ("a predicate on (procedure, problem) pairs, consulting the reachable points' cf's" — Definition 20) is exactly the type at which a counterfactual theory can be judged by such a book. Compare Remark 4.3: there the agent plays against its own statistics (the predictor samples $C(d)$); here the bookie plays with the agent's own labels ($c$, $e$, $P(a)$ — "The agent knows everything the bookie knows"). Both are label-honesty problems (Remark 5.1): the bet's payoff formula embeds $c = V^a_{s_{d'}}(a)$, a component of the *state* at $d'$, and the price threshold embeds $C(d)(a)$ — label-dependence that a concrete tree can carry only as a constant, whose honesty ("this constant *is* the state's cdt") is an abstract-level stipulation.

## 5. Smoking Lesion, the tickle defence, and mixed-strategy ratifiability

**Smoking Lesion.** The post mentions it once, in the normative/descriptive passage: "it's hard to justify smoking lesion as a situation which a CDT or EDT agent could actually find itself in; but, a CDTer might reply, a decision theory needs to offer the right advice to a broad variety of agents." Three things the post contributes, seen from v2. (i) The book, run on the *stipulated* (S2) state, Dutch-books classical CDT ($\Delta = 3/8$ at either act with FA-18's numbers) and leaves EDT unbookable [checked, P12-8] — so "you can't consistently put CDT into Smoking Lesion" (Remark 3.13) has a second, coherence-flavoured sense the post supplies: CDT-with-(S2) is incoherent across a bet and an act, whatever the statistics. (ii) The two diagnostics are orthogonal: EDT-with-(S2) is coherent and, by Proposition 11, holds no realizable worldview and forgoes $\alpha$ per run; CDT-with-(S2) is incoherent and takes the $V$-optimal act. Only at a calibrated point (Proposition 12's tickle instantiation) are coherence and optimality jointly available, and there FA-20′(iii) makes cf = conditioning at positive acts for every $O_d$-conditioned referent — both evaluators smoke. (iii) The "broad variety of agents" defence is answered more strongly by v2 than by the post: $\Sigma_{SL}$ under (S3) is inconsistent for *every* procedure (Proposition 11), so there is no agent, causal or evidential, whose worldview the classic problem describes; the post's answer (CDT is judged on its recommendation to uncertain agents) presupposes an agent who can be in the situation. For L1/C1's fairness grid: the row "SL S3 with the (S2) state" should carry, beside "inconsistent for every $C$" (Proposition 11), the entries "Dutch-bookable for $\mathrm{CDT}$ with the Lewis cf; unbookable for $\mathrm{EDT}$" — the first grid cell where incoherence and miscalibration come apart.

**The tickle defence.** Thesis (4) — "Something can't correlate with your action unless it contains information you don't have about your action" — is the tickle defence generalized from introspected inclinations to *any* correlate, and in v2 it is Lemma 3 under Definition 6: given the node, the draw is independent of everything upstream, so a predictor covaries with the act only through the label the calibrated state already knows (Proposition 7; Appendix B's self-transparency). The post's own scope restriction ("EDT one-boxes with a perfect predictor") is weaker than v2's: under independent redraws even a perfect predictor is screened off at every $O_d$-conditioned evaluation [checked, P12-13, §J: $(19/5, 24/5)$ for the one-boxer's trembles at $p = 1$], and one-boxing at the local level is available only under shared seed — the predictor "containing information you don't have" is, tree-side, the predictor sharing your seed (FA-24′(c)). Two consequences for L2. First, the tension the sequence-map flagged as Q-D is sharpened: P03's Law of Logical Causality (predictors downstream, i.e. covarying with the act under the counterfactual) is the deviation/shared-seed referent, and P12's book *penalizes* it at every $O_d$-conditioned point under Definition 6 (P12-17) — the sequence's strong tickle defence and its Dutch book agree under shared seed and disagree under independent redraws. Second, the classic worry that the tickle defence needs self-knowledge while the book needs self-uncertainty (Lewis's fn 8, as transcribed in `references/INDEX.md` §2.2 from `lewis-1981-causal-decision-theory.txt:312–319`: "It is hard to see how such an agent can be uncertain what he is going to choose, hence hard to see how he can be in a position to deliberate"; and Lewis's *Why Ain'cha Rich* p. 380, read at `…txt:157–166`: "if the subject is still deliberating, then he is not yet sure (even implicitly) what he will do … It is therefore inadmissible to suppose him to be deliberating, and yet suppose him already to be certain") is dissolved in v2 by *mixture* self-knowledge: a properly mixed calibrated state knows $C(d)$ and not the draw, so Lemma 3 screens off *and* every supported act has positive probability — the tickle machinery and the book run at one point (P12-11). For deterministic procedures v2 offers masking (which deletes the tickle resource, Remark 3.9) or trembles (which keep it and turn the book at null acts into the residue, P12-15).

**Mixed-strategy ratifiability.** The post relies on it without naming it: $P(\mathrm{Act}{=}a)$ is "something the agent also knows" (self-transparency of the mixture), (A2) says the equilibrium survives the doubling of the action set (ratifiability of the mixture in $B'$), and the collapse concession ("if $P(A) = 1$, then we had better have $P(X\mid do(A)) = P(X)$") is Skyrms 1990 §6's "self-knowledge probabilizes Newcomb" in the deterministic case (read at `skyrms-1990-ratifiability-…txt:372–381` via INDEX §2.2's transcription; the Nice-Demon passage read directly at `:215–262`). What the post adds to C2's theorem: at a mixed calibrated fixed point the strictly calibrated evidential values tie across the support (Remark 4.3; CA-13′ for the domain caveat), and the book forces any $\mathrm{cf}$ to agree with them at every supported act — so $\mathrm{CDT}$'s ratifiable mixtures are $\mathrm{EDT}$'s [checked on the miniature, P12-6: the unbookable $T_{\mathrm{CDT}}$-approved strictly calibrated pairs are exactly $\{(q{=}2/3, (2/3, 2/3)), (q{=}1, (0,0)), (q{=}0, (0,0))\}$, the supports of CA-13′'s set; with the residue pinning null acts to R3, only $2/3$]. This is P05's "mixed-strategy ratifiability implies CDT=EDT" with the Bayes-net implementability assumption replaced by a pragmatic coherence requirement, and it inherits P05's scope: it is a theorem about the *local* fixed points; the prior-level (updateless) evaluators are untouched (P12-7). Two literature notes for C2: Skyrms's Nice Demon shows a Dutch book *inside* deliberation that forbids raising zero probabilities ("Coherence requires that the initial zero probabilities not be raised. Otherwise the decision-maker is open to a trivial dutch book", `:250–256`) — the mirror image of P12's residue, which wants null acts *priced* by tremble limits without raising their probability; v2's Definition 10 is exactly the device that does the second without the first. And Oesterheld & Conitzer's Anti-Randomization Adversarial Offer (read, `:317–340`) is the case where no mixed act is ratifiable because the seller punishes randomizing — in v2 an environment reading whether the agent randomizes is representable only if the mixture is an event, which Definition 6 does not provide (the tree sees draws, not labels; Remark 5.1) — so v2's mixed-strategy ratifiability is immune to that defeater by type, at the price of not modelling it.
