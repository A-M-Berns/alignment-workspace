## 1. The debate, reconstructed from the sources

Register for this section: everything marked READ was read in the named file at the named lines by this thread; everything from Spohn 1977, Levi, Joyce 2002, Eells 1984, Schick 1979 is [reconstructed] through the quoting source named, and the attribution stands or falls with that source. ### 1.1 The theses (Rabinowicz's two, plus three the corpus forces one to distinguish)

Rabinowicz 2002 (READ in full, `references/rabinowicz-2002-does-practical-deliberation-crowd-out-self-prediction.txt`, no page numbers; `:14–16`): "Weak Thesis: In a situation of choice, an agent does not assign extreme probabilities, one or zero, to options among which his choice is being made. Strong Thesis: In a situation of choice, an agent does not assign any probabilities at all to options among which his choice is being made." He attributes the weak thesis, in various versions, to "Shackle, Ginet, Pears, Goldman, Jeffrey, Schick and Levi" (`:18`) and the strong thesis to "Wolfgang Spohn and Isaac Levi (cf. Spohn (1977) and (1978), Levi (1989), (1991), and (1997)…)" (`:20`). Two scope clarifications he records: the strong thesis "only concerns unconditional probabilities for actions. It does not exclude that the deliberating agent might assign conditional probabilities to his acts" (fn iv, `:374`), and it "extends to all events that are probabilistically dependent on the agent's current options" (ibid.; the formula $P(A) = [P(E) - P(E/\neg A)]/[P(E/A) - P(E/\neg A)]$ at `:376`). It is meant for *current* options only: Levi, quoted at `:134`, allows credal probabilities about "how he will choose at stage 2", and Spohn 1999 (per Rabinowicz fn xxv, `:420`) allows probabilities "to his future, but not to his present actions".

Hájek 2016 (READ, `references/hajek-2016-deliberation-welcomes-prediction.txt`, preprint pagination) renames the strong thesis: "the DARC Thesis: Deliberation Annihilates Reflexive Credences" (`:181–182`), attributed to "Spohn, Levi, Gilboa, Price, Louise, and others" (`:183–184`), with Spohn 1977 p. 115 quoted: "probably anyone will find it absurd to assume that someone has subjective probabilities for things which are under his control and which he can actualize as he pleases" (`:187–190`). Hájek: "I don't." (`:191`).

The corpus forces three further positions to be kept apart from these two:

- **Spohn 2012's reflexive compromise** (READ, `references/spohn-2012-reversing-30-years-of-discussion.txt`). The 1977 principle is restated — "I have stated this 'no probabilities for acts' principle in Spohn (1977, Sect. 2) since it seemed to me to be more or less explicit in all of the decision theoretic literature … except Jeffrey's EDT (1965/1983); Levi (1986) prominently supports this principle under the slogan 'deliberation crowds out prediction'. The arguments in its favor were critically examined by Rabinowicz (2002) in a most careful way. My present attitude toward the principle is a bit more sophisticated" (`:689–695`) — and its formal consequence is named: "If the decision model is denied to contain probabilities for actions, it must not assume a probability measure $p$ for the whole of $U$. … we must replace the measure $p$ for $U$ by a family $(p_h)_{h \in \times H}$ of probability measures for $W$" (`:696–704`), whence "acts are exogenous". The 2012 sophistication: the *reflexive* decision model has "a probability measure for $\times U$", so "the agent now has beliefs about his own actions and even about his own decision situations" (L4 §1.12 quotes `:1021–1027`), constrained by **(SK)**: "If $\Delta_0 \in D$ is the temporally first decision node, there is a particular $\delta_0 \in \Delta_0$ such that $p(\delta_0) = 1$. This condition of consciousness or self-knowledge has first been stated and accepted by Eells (1982, p. 176)" (`:1064–1067`). Evaluation then runs in the *truncated reduction*, whose probability family "is derived from the measure $p$ of $\hat\delta$ by eliminating the reflexive probability of condition (SK) and all probabilities entailed by it, in particular the probabilities for the actions in $Ac(\Delta_0)$" (`:1189–1190`); "the 'no probabilities for acts' principle has reentered the picture, if only in relation to the variables in $Ac(\Delta_0)$" (`:1197–1199`); "the reflexive model allows for completely defined beliefs or probabilities and does therefore justice to the intuitions of those not accepting restrictions in the domain of probabilities. At the same time, it keeps the essence of the 'no probabilities for acts' and the derived 'acts are exogenous' principle through its reducibility to the associated basic decision model" (`:1212–1215`). On Newcomb: "you were committed to one-box all along, and by deliberating you discover to be so committed all along" (`:376–377`).

- **Herrmann's austerity** (READ, `research/unbounded-embedded/06-related-work/papers/herrmann-2023-naturalizing-decision-theory-thesis.txt`, one paragraph per line). "If Hyperion is already certain about one member of this partition (for example, if $P(\mathrm{Fight}) = 1$), then we follow Skyrms in thinking that, for Hyperion, 'there is no decision problem (i.e. his prior probability that he will choose a certain act is one.)' (p. 74, 1984)" (`:724`); "Whereas Skyrms allows agents to assign probability 0 to acts (and just not factor them into their deliberation), we do not countenance acts with 0 probability. I.e., whereas Skyrms might have an act in the set of possible acts that receives probability 0, on my account this would not even count as an act" (`:727`); the ground: "If an agent is completely certain that something will not be the case, then it should not even be represented in the model" (`:731`). Chapter 3 places this "on a ridge that lies between both camps" (`:1049`): "The camp that wants to outlaw act probabilities violates richness. They leave things (propositions dependent on an agent's acts) out of the domain of an agent's belief. The camp that welcome act probabilities violates austerity. They add additional structure beyond the agent's theory of how the world actually is by appealing to some kind of basic powers (Hájek) or causal/counterfactual structure (Joyce, Louise)" (`:1291`).

- **Welcoming, static and dynamic.** Rabinowicz's conclusion — "Deliberation is much less hostile to self-prediction than one might have thought" (`:283`) — and Hájek's — "Not only am I unconvinced by the arguments for the DARC Thesis; I believe there are strong reasons to reject it outright" (`:1059–1061`) — with the dynamic sub-family: Skyrms's deliberational dynamics (READ, both 1990 papers), Eells's metatickle (via Oesterheld, Khan, Rabinowicz fn xxxv — all READ; Eells himself [reconstructed]), Arntzenius's "mixed decision" (Hájek `:1023–1031` READ: "He understands a 'mixed decision'—a decision to perform one's possible actions with specified probabilities—as having those probabilities at the end of rational deliberation. … So, far from deliberation crowding out option credences, it is largely constituted by such credences"), and Joyce 2012's $P_e$ as reproduced by Levinstein–Soares (`:396–408` READ: "Strictly speaking $P_e$ only tells her what she believes she'll do at the end of deliberation. There remains the further question of what she actually should do").

### 1.2 The arguments for the strong thesis, and the replies

**Spohn's betting argument (Argument I in Rabinowicz).** Spohn 1977 p. 115 as quoted by Rabinowicz `:74` and by Hájek `:820–827`: "The agent's readiness to accept a bet on an act does not depend on the betting odds, but only on his gain. If the gain is high enough to put this act on the top of his preference order of acts, he will accept it, and if not, not." Rabinowicz's reconstruction (`:76–92`): take the bet on $A$ iff $eu(A) + G > eu(B)$, so the betting rate $C/S$ does not exist. His criticism (`:94–98`): the reconstruction "presupposes that the agent is certain that, if he takes the attractive bet on his action, he will perform the action in question", i.e. certainty of one's own rationality, which "is really incompatible with the weak thesis … Spohn's argument for the strong thesis would therefore, on this reading, rest on the denial of the weak thesis". His Argument II (`:104–122`) drops the certainty: the bet offer raises $P(A)$, so "the offer of a bet itself changes the agent's probabilities" — which "proves too much" (`:124`), extending to future acts, unless a "forgetfulness manoeuvre" (`:138–146`) restricts it to simultaneous decisions. Hájek's reply (`:843–845`): "Nowhere does deliberation enter into this argument. So it should apply equally to your future acts … we should reject the identification of credences with fair betting rates, premise 4."

**Levi's betting-rate collapse and vacuity argument (Argument III).** Rabinowicz `:162–176`: "Claim 1: If an agent is certain that he won't perform an option, then this option is not feasible"; "Claim 2: If the agent assigns probabilities to options, then, on pain of incoherence, his probabilities for inadmissible (= irrational) options, as revealed by his betting dispositions, must be zero"; together "Only admissible options are feasible", which Levi rejects because "the applicability of the principles should be nonvacuous" (Levi 1997 pp. 25f as quoted at `:180`). The proof of Claim 2 fuses the bet with the act into four complex options with $eu(b\&A) = eu(A) + G$, $eu(\neg b\&B) = eu(B)$, so the bet on $A$ is accepted even at $G = 0$ and the betting rate is 1 (`:195–210`; arithmetic re-checked in `L3-scratch/l3_miniature_cells.py` §6). Rabinowicz's rebuttal is *complementarity*: "being prepared to do a combination of the actions, and being prepared to do one part in this combination, does not imply that one is prepared to do the other part, considered on its own" (`:216`); the motorist "may be prepared to swerve-and-accelerate … But if he has some doubts as to whether he will swerve if he accelerates, he is not prepared to accelerate" (`:218`). He then grants the retort that simultaneous bet-and-act cannot be pulled apart (`:227`) and concludes: "If we take all probabilities to be equal to betting rates, then we have to admit that the deliberating agent lacks probabilities for the options … the identification of probability assignments with betting rates must be put in question" (`:229`), offering *betting commitments under radical uncertainty* with MMEU, which recover $P(A) = C/S$ (`:236–258`). Hájek's version of Levi's vacuity argument is the eight-premise reconstruction at `:651–668` (READ), and his replies (`:674–741`): premise 2 fails at ties — "she has two options, tied in expected utility … She may, for instance, use a fair coin to make her choice, and as a result she assigns credence ½ to each of them (contra the DARC Thesis)"; premise 5 fails because rational agents "need not be smug"; the step 5→6 fails because she "may nonetheless deliberate: to figure out what the admissible options are!" — "Then the thesis isn't really 'deliberation crowds out prediction (for rational agents)'; instead, it's 'rationality crowds out deliberation'!" (`:719–721`); the step 6→7 fails on the reading of "available" as *able*: "Even when you are certain that you will not perform a given action, you may well be able to perform it" (`:728–729`), and on the doxastic reading premise 2 weakens (`:732–741`). Joyce 2002's two-premise reconstruction is quoted by Herrmann `:1174–1175` (READ; Joyce [reconstructed]): "Premise-1: An agent who assigns probabilities to her present actions is required, on pain of irrationality, to assign a probability of zero to any inadmissible act. Premise-2: Once a deliberating agent assigns a subjective probability of zero to an action she no longer regards it as available for choice." Herrmann takes sides: "I agree with Levi that at the end of deliberation an agent must assign probability 1 to the act she will do" (`:1177`), and "The inference from epistemic impossibility to practical impossibility is valid" (`:1243`); Joyce's escape ("[The decision maker] controls what evidence he has concerning his own actions … A is practically possible even though [the decision maker] is sure he will not change his mind and perform it", Joyce 2002 p. 92 as quoted at `:1252`) "relies on additional structure in the agent's attitudes, that she expects to have no empirical consequences (since she assigns such a belief change probability zero)" (`:1256`), and the whole rescue "all lives in probability 0 events, and, in this sense, violates the austerity condition of naturalism" (`:1289`).

**Spohn's "no useful role" argument.** Spohn 1977 p. 115 as quoted by Rabinowicz `:269` and Hájek `:854–867`: "probabilities for acts play no role in decision making. … The decision maker chooses the act he likes most – be its probability as it may. But if this is so, there is no sense in imputing probabilities for acts to the decision maker." Rabinowicz's three replies (`:275–281`): (i) Spohn "would still need to show that such probabilities would be positively harmful"; (ii) "they might do some good" as guideposts allocating deliberative effort; (iii) deliberation as feedback — "my awareness of an inclination to perform a certain action, A, raises its probability and thereby raises the probability of the state of nature this action would be a reliable evidence for … I face instability in my deliberation process" (Death in Damascus, fn xxxiv), with Eells 1984's screening dynamics in fn xxxv. Spohn's recorded comment (fn xxxvi, `:442`): "in objections (ii) and (iii), decision theory is taken as a theory of the deliberational process. But this is something entirely different. I would think, then, that decision theory is entirely inadequate [for that task] and that there is no adequate theory [for this task] at all." Hájek's reply (`:883–899`): the premise is true of Savage's theory but "Taken at face value, Jeffrey's (1983) evidential decision theory requires probabilities for acts … he understands these conditional probabilities in terms of the usual ratio formula: $P(S_i \& A)/P(A)$. To be sure, I would prefer understanding them as primitive conditional probabilities instead"; and "It seems that causal decision theory also requires option-credences. Consider a case in which you take propositions about what you will do to be correlated with dependency hypotheses … Then, if you don't have option-credences, you cannot have credences in the relevant dependency hypotheses." Herrmann (`:1132–1135`) grants Spohn Savage and notes Jeffrey's $U(A) = v(A)P(A)$ role is "fairly underwhelming"; Joyce's answer (Joyce 2002 pp. 98–99 as quoted at `:1143–1145`): "we need unconditional subjective probabilities for decisions about acts to causally explain action (though not to rationalize it), and (ii) we need Efficacy to explain what it is for an agent to regard acts as being under her control."

### 1.3 The dynamic wing: Skyrms, Eells, Jeffrey, Arntzenius

Skyrms, TARK 1990 (READ, `references/skyrms-1990-dynamic-models-of-deliberation-tark.txt`): calculations of expected utility "should change the probabilities of the decisionmaker's acts in a way consistent with the decisionmaker's aiming at maximizing expected utility, but in a way which falls short of giving acts with non-maximal current expected utility probability zero" (`:100–104`); a rule *seeks the good* iff "(1) it raises the probability of an act only if that act has utility greater than that of the status quo, and (2) it raises the sum of the probabilities of all acts with utility greater than that of the status quo" (`:111–119`); "All dynamical rules which seek the good have the same fixed points. These are the states in which the utility of the status quo is maximal" (`:120–121`); the *state of indecision* is "a probability vector assigning probabilities to each of the $n$ acts … These are to be interpreted as her probabilities now that she will do the act in question at the end of deliberation … the adoption of a mixed strategy can be thought of as a way to turn the state of indecision for its constituent pure acts to stone. We will call a mixed act corresponding to a state of indecision, its default mixed act" (`:149–159`); existence by Brouwer when the adaptive rule $D$ and the informational feedback $I$ are continuous (`:182–188`, Theorem I `:193–196`). Skyrms, "Ratifiability and the Logic of Decision" (READ, `references/skyrms-1990-ratifiability-and-the-logic-of-decision.txt`): deliberation as a process on $[0,1]$ "with the decision-maker being initially unsure at $t_0$ of which act to perform, and finally arriving at probability 1 for some act at $t_1$" (`:158–161`); the positive theorem (`:173–177`) that a chosen act remaining ratifiable on $[x,1)$ maximizes Savage expected utility at the moment of truth; the negative theorem (`:228–230`): "There is no coherent model of deliberation under which the probabilities of the states conditional on the acts change continuously with time, such that for any coherent prior Jeffrey and Savage coincide in the limit", proved by the Nice Demon with the coherence clause "Coherence requires that the initial zero probabilities not be raised. Otherwise the decision-maker is open to a trivial dutch book" (`:250–251`); §6 on self-knowledge — "Self-knowledge of expected utility maximization in a curious way prohibits the existence of values for $x$ and $y$ such that Jeffrey expected value maximization is well defined and conflicts with Savage expected utility maximization" (`:377–380`), "Self-knowledge carries with it a kind of self-reference, and flirts with paradox" (`:383–384`), the Mean Demon where "these assumptions overconstrain the initial probabilities. There is no ratifiable act" (`:396–398`), and the weakened assumption under which "the values of $x$ and $y$ are 2/3 and 1/3 … the only values … consistent with the weakened assumption" (`:399–410`); and the conclusion listing "the consequences of self-knowledge for rational decision-makers" among the topics ratifiability opened (`:435–437`). Skyrms 1980 on the tickle defence, as quoted by Oesterheld (`:500–511` READ): "If the utilities, probabilities, and decision rule determine the action, then it is tantamount to taking which action will be performed as a datum in the evaluation of the alternative courses of action. The whole decision process then threatens to become dangerously self-referential" — with Oesterheld's reply that "He has logical uncertainty … over what decision he will make and this should be enough to evaluate different alternative outputs" (`:518–521`) and Eells 1982 p. 150 as quoted at `:560–566`: "there may still be some uncertainty about which act he will choose, for there may still be some calculation left to do."

Eells's metatickle (Eells 1984 [reconstructed]; Oesterheld `:655–700` READ; Khan `:236–290` READ): the agent conditions on the record of its own expected-value calculations $e_1, \dots, e_n$ so that "$P(\text{money in both boxes} \mid \text{one-box}, e_1, \dots, e_n) \approx P(\text{money in both boxes} \mid e_1, \dots, e_n)$" (Oesterheld `:690–695`); Khan: the metatickle "is a proposition which describes the agent's beliefs and desires" (fn 7, `:250–254`), and "For both Eells and Jeffrey ([12]), it is the agent's ability to anticipate her own choices that screens off the evidential import of her acts for states of the world" (`:277`), Jeffrey 1981 p. 486 quoted: "it is my credences and desirabilities at the end of deliberation that correspond to the preferences in the light of which I act" (`:277–281`). Rabinowicz fn xxxv (`:440`): the option's "evidentiary significance … is gradually screened off by the incoming introspective information concerning the agent's probabilities for options … the option's value as news eventually converges to its expected value as cause. Or so Eells argues." Khan's verdict on Huttegger's reconciliation (`:779–785`): it works "only … under two very specific conditions: (i) the independence dynamics must be specified such that it does not take the shortest path to the manifold, and (ii) the adaptive dynamics and independence dynamics must have a relative speed such that they reach the Eells-Jeffrey manifold at precisely the point of reconciliation."

### 1.4 Lewis and Egan on the constraint

Lewis 1981, fn 8 (READ `:312–319`): "It is hard to see how such an agent can be uncertain what he is going to choose, hence hard to see how he can be in a position to deliberate. … If the agent's introspective data make him absolutely certain of his own credences and values, as they must if the Defence is to work, then no amount of evidence that those data are untrustworthy will ever persuade him not to trust them." Lewis 1981 on zeros (`:480–501` READ): "you will not if you are rational lower $C(AK)$ quite to zero. Let it by all means get very, very small; but very, very small denominators do not make utilities go undefined … rash zeros are one sort of irrationality that is downright impossible." Lewis, "Why Ain'cha Rich?" (`:158–170` READ): "if the subject is still deliberating, then he is not yet sure (even implicitly) what he will do. If he is, for instance if $C(A_1) = 0$ and $C(A_2) = 1$, then his decision problem collapses as described in Jeffrey [3]; in which case the distinction between V-rational and V-irrational actions in his situation is undefined." Egan 2007 (`:665–700`, `:755`, `:770–800` READ): the anti-CDT cases require the agent to "start off confident that he will not press the button", but "so too do the examples, like The Smoking Lesion, that motivate CDT over EDT. For those cases to succeed, the agents mustn't be certain of what they're going to choose"; deniers of act-credences are listed in fn 13 ("Levi 1997, Kyburg 1988, Gilboa 1994, and Spohn 1977"); and the way out is Hájek's: "What they ought to do is deny that the standard formula for conditional credences is correct … (Price (1986), Edgington (1995), and Hajek (2003), for example, are all advocates of separating, to some extent, $c(B|A)$ from $c(AB)/c(A)$)."

### 1.5 Hájek on conditional probability (the "related points")

Hájek 2003, "What Conditional Probability Could Not Be" (READ in windows, `research/unbounded-embedded/06-related-work/papers/hajek-what-conditional-probability-could-not-be.txt`): the ratio formula "comes with a proviso: the condition must have positive probability … The ratio analysis fails to give values to certain conditional probabilities that apparently are well-defined. Let us call this the zero denominator problem" (`:227`); on a free act: "why not leave 'Toss' as a probability gap?" (`:399`); "Or following Spohn (1977), Kyburg (1988), Gilboa (1994), and Levi (1997) among others, perhaps you think that there is no obstacle to assigning probability to the free choices of other people, but that you cannot assign probability to your own free choices … Then let us trivially modify the example so that it is you, rather than I, who chooses whether or not to toss the coin. The gap returns" (`:406`); and, decisive for the mapping below: "The 'supervaluational' approach essentially tries to identify absence of probability with presence of vague probability. They are not the same thing" (`:427`). So the 2003 Hájek grants the *gap* for one's own free act as rationally permitted (not required), and holds that a gap is not an imprecise credence; the 2016 Hájek denies that deliberation *requires* the gap. The two are consistent: gaps permitted, DARC denied.

**L3-1 [derived].** The literature's positions on own-act probability are five, not two, and the corpus keeps them apart by their formal consequence: (T0) Strong Thesis / DARC — no unconditional own-act probabilities (Spohn 1977, Levi; [reconstructed] via Rabinowicz `:14–20`, Hájek `:181–190`); (T1) Weak Thesis — no extreme own-act probabilities during choice (Rabinowicz `:14`; Skyrms's positivity `:100–104`); (T2) austerity — a probability-zero act is not an act (Herrmann `:727`, `:731`); (T3) reflexive compromise — total probability including acts, self-knowledge (SK) imposed, evaluation in a reduction with acts exogenous (Spohn 2012 `:1064–1067`, `:1189–1215`); (T4) welcoming — own-act credences rational and useful, statically (Rabinowicz, Hájek, Joyce 2002 [reconstructed]) or as the variable of a deliberation dynamics (Skyrms, Eells 1984 [reconstructed], Arntzenius via Hájek `:1023–1031`, Joyce 2012 via Levinstein–Soares `:396–408`). The disagreements inside T4 (whether the endpoint is $P = 1$ — Skyrms `:158–161`, Herrmann `:1177` — or a mixed equilibrium — Arntzenius, Joyce 2012) matter for §3.

## 4. Verdict for L5: does v2 represent this debate better or worse than the literature's own frameworks?
