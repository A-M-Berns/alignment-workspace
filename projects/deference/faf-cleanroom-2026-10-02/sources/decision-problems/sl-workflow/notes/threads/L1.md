### 1.2 The academic formulations

**F-1 (Gibbard–Harper 1976/1978, Solomon; Case 2).** READ from the scan, typescript pp. 18–19 (PDF pp. 19–20) and pp. 21–23 (PDF pp. 22–24; typescript p. 20 is missing from the scan). Statement (typescript p. 18): "Solomon faces a situation like David's, but he, unlike David, has studied works on psychology and political science which teach him the following: Kings have two basic personality types, charismatic and uncharismatic. A king's degree of charisma depends on his genetic make-up and early childhood experiences, and cannot be changed in adulthood. Now charismatic kings tend to act justly and uncharismatic kings unjustly. Successful revolts against charismatic kings are rare, whereas successful revolts against uncharismatic kings are frequent. Unjust acts themselves, though, do not cause successful revolts; the reason that uncharismatic kings are prone to successful revolts is that they have a sneaky, ignoble bearing. Solomon does not know whether or not he is charismatic; he does know that it is unjust to send for another man's wife." The decision-point correlation (p. 18–19): "if Solomon learned that B, he would have reason to think that he was uncharismatic, and thus revolt-prone. Hence prob (R/B) > prob (R), and therefore prob(B □→ R/B) = prob(R/B) > prob(R) = prob (B □→ R). (6)" Payoffs: David's Matrix 1 (typescript p. 12, READ) — A (abstain): RB̄ 0, R̄B̄ 9; B (take): RB 1, R̄B 10. Verdict (p. 19): "a 𝒰-maximizer will choose to send for his neighbor's wife whereas a 𝒱-maximizer will choose to abstain … Suppose the difference between these probabilities is greater than 1/9, so that where prob (R/A) = α and prob (R/B) = α + ε, we have ε > 1/9." Attributes: (a) Solomon is ignorant of his charisma (the common cause) and nothing is said about his knowledge of his own desires or decision rule; (b) neither — the same agent's credences are fed to two utility notions; (c) **both**: population-level lore ("uncharismatic kings … frequent") *and* the decision-point conditional prob(R/B) > prob(R/A), asserted directly of Solomon's rational credences; (d) no predictor; (e) 0/9/1/10, threshold ε > 1/9.

**L1-2 [checked; `L1-scratch/l1_thresholds.py`].** With Matrix 1, $\mathcal V(A) = 9(1-\alpha)$ and $\mathcal V(B) = 10 - 9(\alpha+\varepsilon)$, so $\mathcal V(A) > \mathcal V(B)$ iff $\varepsilon > 1/9$ — Gibbard–Harper's threshold is exact, and $\mathcal U(B) - \mathcal U(A) = 1$ for every $\alpha$ (dominance, as they say at p. 19).

**F-2 (Lewis 1981, the medical Newcomb problems).** READ `lewis-1981-causal-decision-theory.txt:236–276` (p. 9–10). "Even if the difference of C(E/G) and C(E/−G) is quite small, provided that it exceeds g/e, we have a counterexample. More moderate versions can also be more down-to-earth, as witness the medical Newcomb problems. Suppose you like eating eggs, or smoking, or loafing when you might go out and run. You are convinced, contrary to popular belief, that these pleasures will do you no harm at all. … But also you think you might have some dread medical condition: a lesion of an artery, or nascent cancer, or a weak heart. If you have it, there's nothing you can do about it now and it will probably do you a lot of harm eventually. In its earlier stages, this condition is hard to detect. But you are convinced that it has some tendency, perhaps slight, to cause you to eat eggs, smoke, or loaf. So if you find yourself indulging, that is at least some evidence that you have the condition and are in for big trouble. But is that any reason not to indulge in harmless pleasures? The V-maximising rule says yes, if the numbers are right. I say no." Then the **Tickle Defence** (`:277–297`) and Lewis's reply (`:298–327`), quoted in `INDEX` §2.2; the operative sentence for L1: "A partly rational agent may very well be in a moderate Newcomb problem, either because his choices are influenced by something besides his beliefs and desires or because he cannot quite tell the strengths of his beliefs and desires before he acts." Attributes: (a) the condition is "hard to detect"; the agent's self-knowledge is the *variable* Lewis argues over — the fully rational agent knows his beliefs and desires (tickle), the partly rational one does not, or is moved by something other than them; (b) neither; the agent is "you" with a credence function; (c) decision-point: C(E/G) − C(E/−G) > g/e is a condition on the agent's own conditional credences; population statistics enter only as their source; (d) none; (e) schematic: small good g, great evil e.

**F-4 (Egan 2007: Susan; Murder Lesion; Psychopath Button; the modified Smoking Lesion).** READ `egan-2007-counterexamples-to-cdt.txt:66–84, 109–113, 262–310, 334–400, 615–637, 673–700, 750–770`. Susan (`:66–80`): "Susan is debating whether or not to smoke. She knows that smoking is strongly correlated with lung cancer, but only because there is a common cause – a condition that tends to cause both smoking and cancer. **L1-3 [checked; `L1-scratch/l1_thresholds.py`].** Briggs's joint credence is exactly the population law of a two-type tickle instantiation of v2's (S1) in which the lesioned always smoke and the unlesioned never do: $\rho = \tfrac12$, $\gamma_1 = .74$, $\gamma_0 = .26$ reproduce $C(S\wedge R)=.37$, $C(S\wedge\neg R)=.13$, $C(\neg S\wedge R)=.13$, $C(\neg S\wedge\neg R)=.37$ and hence $V_E(S)=-64$, $V_E(\neg S)=-26$, $V_C(S)=-40$, $V_C(\neg S)=-50$ with $V_C$ read at the unconditional $C(R)=\tfrac12$. So Briggs's Susan *is* the population of Proposition 12's instantiation — her credence is $\nu$ marginalized over the lesion — and the decision-point reading of that credence (Susan herself has $P_s(R\mid S) = .74$) is exactly what Lemma 3 forbids at any calibrated point (§2 below).

**F-6 (Spohn 2012, three smoker's stories).** READ `spohn-2012-…txt:329–354`. "The true story is, of course, that smoking causes, or raises the risk of lung cancer … There is a well-known alternative story. Smoking as such is harmless. There is, however, a gene, the smoker's gene, that probabilistically causes both, lung cancer and the desire to smoke. Should you smoke, then, if you have the desire? Even though you are likely then to have the gene and to develop lung cancer? Yes, of course, CDT says; there is no use whatsoever in abstaining from smoking in that scenario. It corresponds to (NP1∗). Now consider a third variant. There, perversely, the desire to smoke itself causes lung cancer not via smoking, but via some other causal route; thus, the desire is a common cause of smoking and cancer. The scenario corresponds to (NP2∗). Knowing these causal relations, should you smoke? Certainly not. Given these side effects, you should not even have the desire to smoke and hence not smoke; and if you are rational, this is what you do and what your desires are, judged again from the stance of CDT." And: "I do not advise the person with the smoker's gene to abstain from smoking." Attributes: (a) the agent *has* the desire (knows it: "if you have the desire") — Spohn's reflexive decision graphs contain the decision node as a variable the agent can reflect on; (b) the agent is a (reflexive) CDT agent; (c) the second story is population-level with the desire known; the third makes the *decision situation itself* the common cause — a label-level dependence (§2.9 below); (d) none; (e) none.

**F-7 (SEP, Weirich 2024, the generic medical Newcomb problem).** READ `sep-causal-decision-theory.md:127`: "In realistic, medical Newcomb problems, a medical condition and a behavioral symptom have a common cause and are correlated although neither causes the other. If the behavior is attractive, dominance recommends it although expected-utility maximization prohibits it." Attributes: purely population-level; no self-knowledge stipulation; no numbers; the two rules (dominance vs conditional-probability expected utility) are compared on one agent.

### 1.3 The MIRI / LessWrong formulations

**F-8 (Yudkowsky–Soares 2017, Dilemma 3; the formalized version; the verdict).** READ `yudkowsky-soares-2017-fdt.txt:166–190, 1160–1310, 1707–1745`. Statement (`:168–182`): "An agent is debating whether or not to smoke. She knows that smoking is correlated with an invariably fatal variety of lung cancer, but the correlation is (in this imaginary world) entirely due to a common cause: an arterial lesion that causes those afflicted with it to love smoking and also (99% of the time) causes them to develop lung cancer. There is no direct causal link between smoking and lung cancer. Agents without this lesion contract lung cancer only 1% of the time, and an agent can neither directly observe nor control whether she suffers from the lesion. The agent gains utility equivalent to $1,000 by smoking (regardless of whether she dies soon), and gains utility equivalent to $1,000,000 if she doesn't die of cancer. Should she smoke, or refrain?" Then (`:183–190`): "the CDT agent smokes. The EDT agent forgoes the free 1,000 utility and studiously avoids smoking". The formalization (`:1160–1200`): Eve's $P^E$ is Newcomb's distribution under the renaming Predisposition→Lesion, Accurate→Luck, Prediction→Cancer, Box B→Death, twobox→smoke, onebox→refrain — "Clearly, then, Eve refrains from smoking … for the same reason that it was onebox in Newcomb's problem"; Fiona (`:1216–1290`) has two utility functions $U_S, U_R$, two distributions $P_S, P_R$, "Fiona can't tell which distribution she actually uses; it's a black box to her", $\mathrm{Act}(\mathrm{lesion}, s, r) := s$, $\mathrm{Act}(\mathrm{nolesion}, s, r) := r$, and "computes her action under ignorance about her own preferences"; she smokes, by $p\cdot\$1{,}000$. **The verdict** (`:1293–1310`): "Eells (1984) has objected that when one fully formalizes this problem, as we have, it is revealed to be quite contrived—the agents are asked to make their choice under uncertainty about their own desires, which seems unrealistic. We agree, and note that the problem is more unfair to EDT than is generally supposed: Eve's distribution $P^E$ lies to her! If Eve lives in a population of EDT agents, then none of them smoke, and so there should be no correlation between Act and Lesion. To make $P^E$ accurate, we would need Eve to live in a population of agents that use a different decision procedure than she does, while also demanding that Eve be ignorant of this fact (as well as her own desires). The discussion above reveals that the smoking lesion problem is badly confused. … we recommend that philosophers abandon the dilemma." Fairness (`:1721–1726`): "FDT does appear to be superior to CDT and EDT in all dilemmas where the agent's beliefs are accurate and the outcome depends only on the agent's behavior in the dilemma at hand. Informally, we call these sorts of problems 'fair problems.'" Attributes: (a) the lesion is unobservable ("can neither directly observe nor control"); in the formalization the agent is ignorant of her own *utility function* (Fiona) or *predisposition* (Eve); the agent knows her decision procedure (she is Eve/Carl/Fiona by construction); (b) yes — the agent is explicitly an EDT/CDT/FDT agent; (c) population-level in the story, decision-point in $P^E$ (Eve's conditionals are the population's); (d) none; (e) $1,000 / $1,000,000; 99% / 1%.

**L1-4 [derived].** The FDT paper's "$P^E$ lies to her … If Eve lives in a population of EDT agents, then none of them smoke, and so there should be no correlation between Act and Lesion" is Proposition 11's inconsistency stated in the authors' words, for the special case of a deterministic evidential population: with every run's act drawn from the same label, the population correlation the stipulated state reports cannot be realized. v2 generalizes it in two ways the paper does not: the inconsistency holds for *every* procedure, causal included ("inconsistent for everyone"), and it survives the masked grade — no full-support self-model rescues (S2), because Lemma 3 runs on the tree's sampling, not on the agent's decision rule. What the paper has that v2 lacks: the *third* population — "agents that use a different decision procedure than she does" — is representable in v2 only by a mixture over instantiations (Appendix B item 4, mixture calibration), and v2's own check (change-log v2→v2.1 item 19) says the inconsistency survives mixing; so the paper's escape route ("Eve … ignorant of this fact") is closed in v2 as well — Eve would have to be *miscalibrated* about which population she is in, which is Definition 8's clause 1 failing on the event "my procedure is EDT" if the algebra events it (Q1).

**F-9 (Yudkowsky 2010, Chewing-Gum / CGTA; Newcomb's Soda).** The TDT paper is **not in the corpus**; CGTA is READ secondhand from Drescher's summary, `eaforum-summary-ahmed-…md:483–496`: "a recently published medical study shows that chewing gum seems to cause throat abscesses – an outcome-tracking study showed that of people who chew gum, 90% died of throat abscesses before the age of 50. Meanwhile, of people who do not chew gum, only 10% die … a second study … shows that most gum-chewers have a certain gene, CGTA": CGTA present — chew 89% die, don't 99%; CGTA absent — chew 8%, don't 11%; "natural selection has produced in them a tendency to chew gum, which protects against throat abscesses." Note the structure: gum is *protective* (a causal effect of the act) while the gene raises both gum-chewing and abscesses — a mixed case in Lewis's sense (`lewis-…txt:271–276`: "taking the good has some tendency to produce (or prevent) the evil, but also is a manifestation of some prior state"). Drescher reports Yudkowsky's §9.1 resolution as a tickle argument (the gene works through desires, so an unusual desire is the evidence and the decision adds nothing). **Newcomb's Soda** is [reconstructed, unverified]: no file in the corpus contains it (`grep -i soda` over references/ returns nothing); from memory it is Yudkowsky 2010's variant in which a randomly assigned soda (chocolate or vanilla) causes both a later ice-cream choice and a monetary payoff tied to the soda, so that the ice-cream choice is evidence of the soda — I do not rely on it below. Attributes (CGTA): (a) nothing stipulated about introspection (Drescher: "more open-ended because it doesn't give away the agent's desire in the setup"); (b) neither; (c) population (two studies); (d) none; (e) the four death rates.

**F-10 (Levinstein & Soares 2020: the refusal, and Murder Lesion à la Joyce 2012).** READ `levinstein-soares-2020-…txt:172–175, 993–1060, 1085–1160`. fn 1: "We use this case [XOR Blackmail] … instead of the more familiar Smoking Lesion from Skyrms (1980) for two reasons. First, the smoking lesion problem requires the agent to be uncertain about their own desires. Second, the present case is immune to the so-called 'tickle defense' (Eells 1984)." Murder Lesion (`:993–1005`, "discussed at length in Joyce (2012)"): "you are a random member of a population in which 20% of people have a brain lesion that both fortifies their nerve and causes their hands to tremble when they shoot. Eight in ten people who have the lesion can bring themselves to shoot, but they invariably miss. Those who lack the lesion shoot only one time in 10, but always hit their targets." Payoffs (Table 2): $S$: $L\ {-30}$, $\neg L\ 10$; $\neg S$: 0, 0. CDT by Joyce's deliberational dynamics (`:1085–1095`): "no matter how strong the correlation between shooting and shaky hands, cdt agents will shoot with 25% probability … on average receive a payoff of −7.5." FDT (`:1101–1114`): "the arrow between the lesion and fdt(p,g) would imply that the presence or absence of the lesion affects the output of the (fixed) fdt equations, which is false … the official description of the case leaves underspecified exactly how the lesion and fdt jointly end up affecting the action. Thus the case is underspecified from the point of view of the functional decision theorist … Critically, this is not true of a causal decision theorist who accepts Figure 6 as a full specification." Their specification (`:1117–1146`): with the lesion you are hard-wired with $\mathrm{Alg}_s$ or $\mathrm{Alg}_n$ (auto-shoot / auto-abstain with probability $y$, else defer to fdt); FDT shoots because "insofar as fdt has control … it should only take into account the initial probability that you have the lesion". Attributes: (a) the agent knows the population statistics and her procedure, not her lesion; (b) yes, CDT / FDT agents by construction; (c) population ("a random member of a population") — the decision-point correlation is *derived* by the CDT agent's deliberation and then declared decision-irrelevant; (d) none; (e) −30/10/0/0, 20%, 8/10, 1/10.

**L1-5 [derived].** Levinstein–Soares's "underspecified" verdict and their repair are, in v2's terms, the statement that the lesion's route to the act is not a chance draw *upstream of the point* but a device that overrides the *label*: $\mathrm{Alg}_s/\mathrm{Alg}_n$ "automatically returns shoot with probability $y$ regardless of what fdt says". That is exactly the **post-decision tremble** structure of P03 ("more like a trembling hand after the decision is made") and of §2.7 below: the realized act is a chance function of (lesion, the point's draw), so it correlates with the lesion at a calibrated point while the point's own draw does not. Their reading is thus one determinate instantiation of a stipulation set that the story leaves open — Definition 14's picture, in their words ("different formulations of the details can yield different problems, and thus different correct solutions"). **F-11 (Ahmed 2014, medical Newcomb problems — summary only).** READ secondhand from `eaforum-summary-ahmed-…md:437–461`: Susan (p. 90) verbatim Egan; the mechanism hypothesis (p. 93): "The more plausible hypothesis is that the lesion produces smoking via its effect on the agent's desires and beliefs"; the three-step argument (p. 91): "(4.7) Mental causation: the undesirable event produces the dominant option via and only via its operation upon the agent's current predecision desires and beliefs. ## 3. Fairness verdicts (Task 3)

Six senses of "fair", each named at every use (the phase-2 vocabulary of P2:identity ID-27, plus two from the mandate):

- **F1 decision-determined** (Soares 2017 `soares-2017-…md:122`: dilemmas "evaluate agents based on their *actions*"; Yudkowsky–Soares `:1721–1723`: "the outcome depends only on the agent's behavior in the dilemma at hand"; ID-26's criterion: the external policy is the only agent-intrinsic quantity that impacts the environment).
- **F2 honest at grade $\kappa$** (Definition 15: some instantiation is $\kappa$-calibrated for $C$; always name $\kappa$ and $C$).
- **F3 located / dislocated / relocatable** (ID-27; P1:fair-repair): whether the point sits at one node per branch structure, and whether relocation is state-innocent (DL-9r).
- **F4 inconsistency-making** (Definition 16): whether a procedure makes an event inconsistent in $\Sigma$ — Soares's "decisions are for making bad outcomes inconsistent".
- **F5 Lewis's partly-rational-agent sense** (`lewis-1981-…txt:298–306`): is the problem *possible* for an agent who "cannot quite tell the strengths of his beliefs and desires" or "whose choices are influenced by something besides his beliefs and desires"? In v2 the first clause is the masked grade (Definition 9 frees exactly the self-model; Remark 3.9 says masking "deletes the resource the tickle defence runs on"), the second is a post-decision tremble (L1-20).
- **well-posed** (ID-27's composite).

**L1-23 [derived from §2].** *The classic under (S3)* — every formulation that asserts the population correlation as the agent's own act-conditional at a lesion-uninformative point (F-1's eq. (6), F-2's $C(E/G) - C(E/{-G}) > g/e$, F-4 Susan, F-5, F-8's $P^E$, F-13, F-14; the SEP's F-7 read that way). F1: fair (decision-determined; cancer reads the lesion and nothing agent-intrinsic beyond the act). F2: **dishonest at every grade for every procedure** — strict, masked, limit, per-run, per-occurrence (L1-7); this is the sense in which "Smoking Lesion is unfair", and it is not procedure-relative: there is no "for whom". F3: dislocated (two $d$-nodes) and relocatable state-innocently (L1-8) — relocation is irrelevant to (S2). F4: no procedure makes cancer less likely ($\nu(k)$ is deviation-invariant, L1-10): the refrainer aims at an event no decision can touch, which is Soares's diagnosis of the refrainer read through Definition 16. F5: **unfair to Lewis's partly rational agent in the first sense** — masking does not restore the lesion's evidential relevance (Proposition 11 covers the masked grade), so an agent whose only defect is not knowing her own inclinations is *still* not in a Newcomb problem via the lesion; Lewis's "partly rational agent may very well be in a moderate Newcomb problem … because he cannot quite tell the strengths of his beliefs and desires" is **refuted at the masked grade under (S1)+(S3) with independent redraws** — the correlation runs through the label, and the label is what masking frees. His second route ("influenced by something besides his beliefs and desires") survives as L1-20's tremble, at the price of the action not being the agent's draw. Well-posed: **no** — an inconsistency-making description.

**L1-24 [derived from §2].** *The tickle instantiation* (F-3, F-6 story 2, F-11, F-14's "Can't they just check whether they enjoy smoking?").
