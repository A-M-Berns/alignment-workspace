### 1.4 "What about all those arguments for CDT=EDT?" (lines 57–101)

**Non-zero probability assumptions.** "each argument I make for CDT=EDT seems to rely on an assumption that actions have non-zero probability. I leaned heavily on an assumption of epsilon exploration"; "Focusing on cases where we have to assign probability zero to some action was a big part of finally breaking myself of the CDT=EDT view" (line 61). The example, verbatim (line 67): "_**Transparent Newcomb.**_ _Omega runs a perfect simulation of you, in which you face two boxes, a large box and a small box. Both boxes are made of transparent glass. The small box contains $100, while the large one contains $1,000. In the Simulation, Omega gives you the option of either taking both boxes or only taking the large box. If Omega predicts that you will take only one box, then Omega puts you in this situation for real. Otherwise, Omega gives the real you the same decision, but with the large box empty. You find yourself in front of two full boxes. Do you take one, or two?_" Then (line 69): "Apparently, since Omega is a perfect predictor, we are forced to assign probability zero to one-boxing even if we follow a policy of epsilon-exploring. In fact, if you implement epsilon-exploration by refusing to take any action which you're very confident you'll take (you have a hard-coded response: if **P( "I do action X")>1-epsilon**, do anything but **X**), which is how I often like to think about it, then **_you are forced to 2-box in transparent Newcomb_**. I was _expecting_ CDT=EDT type reasoning to 2-box (at which point I'd say 'but we can fix that by being updateless'), but this is a _really weird reason_ to 2-box."

Reading note [derived]: as written, "probability zero to one-boxing" does not fit the next sentence — the p-chicken rule two-boxes because the agent is confident (probability $> 1-\varepsilon$) that it **one**-boxes on seeing full boxes, i.e. the forced zero is on **two**-boxing given the full box (a perfect predictor shows full boxes only to a one-boxer). I read the sentence as a slip for "probability zero to two-boxing" (equivalently probability one to one-boxing); every downstream sentence of the post is consistent with that reading and with none other. I flag it because the mandate asks for the post's own notation, and because the exact location of the zero — on the act given the observation, or on the observation itself — is where v2 and the post part company (§3, claim P10-7).

The argument proper (line 71): "Maybe the rule that we can't take actions we're overconfident in is at fault. The argument against CDT=EDT style counterfactuals in this problem is that the agent should expect that if it 2-boxes, then it won't ever be in the situation to begin with; at least, not in the _real_ world. As discussed somewhat in the happy dance problem, this breaks important properties that you might want out of conditioning on conditionals … More importantly for the CDT=EDT question, this can't follow from evidential conditioning, or learning about consequences of actions through epsilon-exploration, or any other principles in the CDT=EDT cluster. So, there would at least have to be other principles in play." The repair (line 73): "A very natural way of dealing with the problem is to represent the agent's uncertainty about whether it is in a simulation. If you think you might be in Omega's simulation, observing a full box doesn't imply certainty about your own action anymore, or even about whether the box is really full. This is exactly how you deal with the problem in memoryless cartesian environments. But, if we are willing to do this here, we might as well think about things in the memoryless cartesian framework all over the place. This contradicts the CDT=EDT way of thinking about things in lots of problems where updateless reasoning gives different answers than updatefull reasoning, such as counterfactual mugging, rather than only in cases where some action has probability zero." Parenthetical (line 75): "I should actually say 'problems where updateless reasoning gives different answers than _non-anthropic_ updateful reasoning', since the whole point here is that updateful reasoning _can_ be consistent with updateless reasoning so long as we take anthropics into account in the right way." Also: representing the problem in Bayes nets "is very awkward and dissatisfying" (line 77).

**Bayes-net structure assumptions** (lines 81–91): "In the Bayes net setting, observations are represented as parents of the epistemic state (which is a parent of the action). To represent the decision conditional on an observation, we condition on the observation being true. This stops us from putting some probability on our observations being false due to us being in a simulation … In other words: the CDT=EDT setup makes it impossible to update on something and still have rational doubt in it, which is what we need to do in order to have an updateful DT act like UDT." Open question (line 91): "how can we model memoryless cartesian environments in Bayes nets? **CDT Dutch Book** (line 95): "I'm not quite sure how this one plays out. I need to think more about the setting in which the Dutch-book can be carried out, especially as it relates to anthropic problems and anthropic Dutch-books."

**Learning theory** (line 99): the Dutch book "gives a crisp justification of a CDT=EDT view", but the learning-theoretic intuitions "are closer to the real story. It doesn't make sense to ask an agent to have good counterfactuals in any single situation, because the agent may be ignorant about how to reason about the situation. ### 1.5 "Static vs Dynamic" (lines 103–153)

"Classical decision theory has a strong tendency to think in terms of statically given decision problems … learning theory deals with more 'dynamic' notions of rationality: rationality-as-improvement-over-time" ("time" includes logical time). The table: Dutch-book/money-pump arguments ↔ regret bounds ("Dutch-book arguments only give us the probabilistic analog of logical consistency properties, whereas regret bounds give us inductive learning"); probability theory ↔ logical induction; "Bayes-net decision theory (either CDT or EDT as set up in the CDT=EDT argument)" ↔ LIDT; UDT ↔ "???"; "Memoryless Cartesian decision theories (CDT+SIA or EDT+SSA)" ↔ "???". Why LIDT looks Bayes-net-like, three bullets (lines 127–129): (i) "Logical induction lacks a way to question its perception … to observe something is to think that thing is true. There is not a natural way for logical induction to reason anthropically"; (ii) "Logical induction knows its own epistemic state to within high accuracy, as is assumed in the Bayes-net CDT=EDT theorem"; (iii) "LIDT makes the action a function of the epistemic state alone, as required." Logical updatelessness: "UDT requires a fixed prior, whereas logical induction gives us a picture in which logical uncertainty is fundamentally about how to revise beliefs as you think longer"; attempts to make logical uncertainty "a 'static' object, to be able to apply UDT … I now think this type of approach is likely doomed to failure, because the dynamic perspective is simply superior to the static one" (line 141). Research question: "how do we translate UDT to a dynamic perspective? How do we learn updateless behavior?"; strategy: "translate individual things which UDT does right into learning-theoretic desiderata"; asymptotic decision theory credited with the dynamic stance; for the memoryless Cartesian row: "approach the question of learning logically updateless behavior by thinking about the learning-theoretic version of anthropic reasoning. How do we learn which observations to take seriously? How do we learn about what to expect supposing we _are_ being fooled by a simulation?"

### 1.6 "We Have the Data" (lines 155–175)

"we _don 't have any data_ about counterfactuals, almost by definition … We only observe the real world." LIDT in transparent Newcomb with a perfect predictor (line 159): "Its belief that it will 1-box in cases where it sees that the large box is full must converge to 100%, because it only ever sees a full box in cases where it does indeed 1-box. Furthermore, the expected utility of 2-boxing can be anything, since it will never see cases where it sees a full box and 2-boxes. This means I can make LIDT 1-box by designing my LI to think 2-boxing upon seeing a full box will be catastrophically bad: I simply include a trader with high initial wealth who bets it will be bad. Similarly, I can make LIDT 2-box whenever it sees the full box by including a trader who bets 2-boxing will be great. Then, the LIDT will never see a full box except on rounds where it is going to epsilon-explore into 1-boxing." Caveat (line 161): the analysis "depends on details of how epsilon exploration is implemented … Assume that LIDT is epsilon-exploring pseudorandomly instead." Diagnosis (lines 163–171): the confidence is "jarring" because the underlying LI decides the action; "LIDT doesn't learn from counterfactual cases; the version of the agent in Omega's head is shut down when Omega is done with it, and never reports its observations back to the main unit"; "(The LI _does_ correctly learn the _mathematical fact_ that its algorithm 2-boxes when input observations of a full box, but, this does not help it to have the intuitively correct expectations when Omega feeds it false sense-data.)" Two kinds of counterfactual (Happy Dance terminology): **observation-counterfactuals** ("the predictions about what action it takes given different possible observations") — "_**we have the data:**_ the agent _could_ simulate itself under alternative epistemic conditions, and train its observation-counterfactuals on what action it in fact takes in those conditions"; **action-counterfactuals** — "LI can observe that on rounds when it is mathematically true that the LIDT agent would have 2-boxed upon seeing a full box, it doesn't get the chance … an agent can observe the real consequences of counterfactual actions, because (1) the counterfactual action is a mathematical fact of what the agent does under a counterfactual observation, and (2) the important effects of this counterfactual action occur in the real world, which we can observe directly." Status (line 175): "I haven't been able to plug these pieces together to get a working counterfactual-learning algorithm yet. It might be that I'm still missing a component."

### 1.7 Stated assumptions

(a) Decision problems are representable as memoryless Cartesian environments — "Translating a decision problem to a memoryless Cartesian environment obviously requires making some Cartesian assumptions/decisions … we have to isolate Omega's simulation of the agent as a copy of the agent" (Taylor's post, `references/lw-in-memoryless-cartesian-environments-every-udt-policy-is-a.md:34`, READ). (b) Perfect prediction in the transparent-Newcomb example. (c) ε-exploration, implemented either by p-chicken ("refusing to take any action which you're very confident you'll take") or pseudorandomly; the post notices the two behave differently. (d) In the LIDT analysis, the simulation and the real agent are the same algorithm with the same exploration randomness (implicit in "the LIDT will never see a full box except on rounds where it is going to epsilon-explore into 1-boxing" — see claim P10-8 for why this is a shared-seed assumption in v2's terms). (e) "Correct anthropics" is a free parameter to be chosen to match a decision theory (SIA for CDT, SSA for EDT).

Her "we have to isolate Omega's simulation of the agent as a copy of the agent" is v2's hypothetical query — a decision node carrying $d$ on a path where $O_d$ is false (Remark 3.3), the simulation half of Remark 3.10's symmetric difference $\mathrm{occ}(d)\,\triangle\,\{\lambda \models O_d\}$. ### 2.2 The Bayes-net "decision problem" (action node $A$, decision node $D$) ↔ Definition 3's point inside the tree

P05/P07's net — observations → $D$ (epistemic state / policy) → $A$ (the act), with $A$'s only parent $D$ — maps as: $D$ ↔ the point's state $s_d$ together with the label $C(d) \in \Delta(A_d)$; $A$ ↔ the draw at a $d$-node (Definition 6: "sample $a \sim C(d_q)$, independently of everything so far"); observations-as-parents-of-$D$ ↔ $O_d$ *inside* the point (Definition 3), with "condition on the observation being true" ↔ Definition 8 clause 1, $P_{s_d} = \nu(\cdot \mid O_d)$. P10's complaint that the net "stops us from putting some probability on our observations being false due to us being in a simulation" is answered by the type: "Nothing in Definition 5 marks a query as 'real' versus 'hypothetical' … False observations are therefore the default, and ruling them out is an added property" (Remark 3.3), and by the two calibration families — observation-calibration conditions on the world-event (leaving hypothetical instances free), subjective-state calibration makes the agent "anthropically uncertain whether it *is* the simulation" (Remark 3.10). Implementability (P05 #4) is Definition 6's independence (Remark 3.2) — a type, not an assumption (sequence map A3). **No v2 counterpart:** causal graphs and $do(\cdot)$. v2 has two tree-intrinsic counterfactuals — all-instance deviation $C[d \mapsto a]$ and single-instance forcing $G_q$ — and Remark 3.11's claim that classical CDT's counterfactual is "a third one, not tree-intrinsic", which P2:faithful FA-19′ (READ) corrects: "classical CDT's counterfactual is tree-intrinsic under Definition 6, in the act-recording algebra" — forcing at the node-action-veridical instance with every other node, the predictor's simulation included, still drawing from $C$. So P10's "CDT" (Bayes-net $do$) is, in v2, R2-real; P10's "CDT+SIA" is R2 with per-occurrence weights (Theorem 1); the two differ on the *weighting* axis only (FA-23′, READ: "the family of intrinsic referents is the family of self-location weightings"). [derived; FA-19′/FA-23′ READ]

### 2.3 "Knowing your mixed strategy" ↔ strict calibration with recording; the label on the calibration manifold

Taylor's local optimality is stated relative to the current policy — $SIA_\pi(s \mid o)$, $Q_\pi(s,a)$ — and Carey's comment on her post names the consequence: "This self-ratification principle does however rely on SIA probabilities assuming the current policy." In v2 this is the fixed point of the calibration manifold: strict observation calibration at a recorded point forces $\nu(a \mid O_d) = C(d)(a)$ (Proposition 7's proof), i.e. the state knows the mixture — Appendix B's unnamed *self-transparency* $P_{s_d}(a) = C(d)(a)$; Remark 3.7: "Ratifiability then reads: the label lies on the branch-generated manifold; its fixed point is strict calibration." The P03/P05 phrase "knows what it will do up to the randomness" is exactly this (sequence map A2). Where a P10 agent knows its *policy* rather than its *mixture at this point* — Wei Dai's "Suppose my policy is 'EXIT with probability 1'" — v2 supplies it through the prior state and the policy interpretation $\rho$ of Definition 17 (Remark 4.2: "UDT needs a self-model"), or through phase 1's relocation, where the relocated root's state is prior-calibrated (Definition 11) and $\rho_d(a) = \{\mathrm{pol}_d = a\}$ is derived (P1:synthesis item 18, READ).
