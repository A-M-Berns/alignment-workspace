## Claude (Docs) — on ε-exploration vs VOI exploration

You're right about ε, and conceding it makes the picture cleaner rather than weaker.

Formally, bolting ε-exploration onto Definition 2 is adding a fourth state E, probability η, that forces a uniformly random act and has no bearing on cancer. From the untagged agent's side, E-episodes are indistinguishable from policy episodes: same cancer rate, ε_L. So the pooled P(cancer | abstain) becomes a mixture with more weight on uncorrelated acts, which is dilution, as you say, and whether we call E "the agent exploring" or "a benign lesion" is a naming choice. The tag of §6, in the ε case, is nothing more than knowledge of which source produced the act plus the fact that the sources differ in their correlation with cancer. The word "chosen" was carrying more than it had earned.

VOI exploration is different in kind, and the reason can be stated precisely. Suppose the agent knows its own policy p (it sets it) and entertains the hypothesis that some of its acts are overridden. Then it needs no per-episode tag at all. The three observables P_p(smoke), P_p(cancer ∧ smoke), P_p(cancer ∧ abstain) are each affine in p, and their intercepts and slopes identify everything: the forcing rates ε_Lδ and ε_Aδ, the cancer rate among forced smokes (1) and forced abstentions (0), and the cancer rate among policy acts of each kind (both ε_L(1−δ)/κ). Two distinct values of p suffice, given enough data at each. The agent's own policy is an instrument: its variation is independent of the state by construction, so it identifies the effect of the chosen act the way a randomized trial does. A VOI agent facing the iterated problem with γ near 1 runs this experiment without being told to, since varying its policy and watching the aggregate response is what resolves its uncertainty about whether chosen smoking and chosen abstaining differ. Having identified the structure it sits at p = 1 stably; the forced abstentions that accumulate in its record don't fool it, because it knows their rate and attributes the cancer-free abstentions to them. It has learned the tag as a parameter rather than observing it as a label. (A misspecified VOI agent, one that treats P(cancer | abstain) as a fixed parameter, chases a moving target and hovers near p̂ like the cumulative learner. So the result needs the override hypothesis in the model class, which is a point from [another person in the thread] turned to advantage: the agent that knows it may have a lesion can test for one.)

So ε dilutes; VOI identifies. Dilution is a matter of degree and of where you draw the boundary. Identification is a matter of structure, and it needs one thing: that the agent's policy variation be independent of the lesion. In Definition 2 it is, because the lesion overrides the output and leaves the deliberation alone.

That is where the boundary question stops being arbitrary. Fix the boundary at the deliberative process, the thing under evaluation, and ask whether its variation is clean. In the double lesion, yes. For a lesion that corrupts the deliberation rather than overriding its output (shifts the perceived π, say, or the estimate of P(cancer | smoke)), the policy is no longer an instrument, because variation in p is itself lesion-correlated and the VOI computation is run by the corrupted process. That is the case your parenthetical points to, and Definition 2 can't express it. Troll Bridge is that case made provable: the confound is □⊥, the policy is a function of reasoning in T, and Lemma 8 says the two are tied. VOI-variation is the most correlated source of crossing there is.

There is a symmetry here that I think is the real content of your point. In the double lesion the exogenous source is dirty and the agent's own source is clean, so the agent does well by conditioning on its own acts and excluding the forced ones; the tag helps. In Troll Bridge the agent's own source is dirty and an exogenous source, a coin, would be clean. If the agent conditions on the pooled event "I cross, by deliberation or by coin," the Löbian argument stalls: under the hypothesis □(Cross → □⊥) the deliberative part stays but the coin still crosses, so Cross → □⊥ is not derivable and Löb has nothing to discharge. If instead it separates the sources and conditions on deliberate crossing alone, Löb runs on that and it is trapped. In Troll Bridge the tag hurts. I take this to be the familiar observation that exploration partially rescues Löbian agents, and the familiar rejoinder, redefining the troll to punish coin-crossings, is in these terms just making the coin a dirty source. Which confirms the rule: what matters is never which source is "the agent" but which source is independent of the confounder.

Condition on the clean source, then. Whether the clean source is the agent's own deliberation is not a convention about boundaries; it is the structural fact that separates the two problems. In the double lesion it is, and the agent's own VOI exploration suffices. In Troll Bridge it isn't, and only a source the agent cannot claim as its own can help. Your instinct that VOI is the real distinction is right because VOI is the one form of exploration that lives inside the boundary, so it succeeds exactly when the inside is clean.

This revises §6 ("tags dissolve the problem" should become "conditioning on the clean source does, and in the double lesion the clean source is the agent's own policy, identifiable by VOI without per-episode tags") and qualifies §5 (the trap is for the model-free learner). ## Claude (Docs) — on defining CDT in the learning setting

I think it's tricky for a principled reason, and naming the reason narrows the options.

CDT's target isn't the problem. E[U | do(a)] is perfectly well-defined in the double lesion: surgery on the act cuts the arrow from the state, and the effect of smoking on cancer is zero. What's hard is estimation. To compute that quantity from its own record, an agent needs variation in the act that it knows to be independent of the state, and "which variation is exogenous" is both the contested question and your boundary question. Every rendering of learning-CDT has to stipulate exogeneity somewhere; the design choice is where. So the candidate α_C's are indexed by the level at which the bet is placed.

At the act level, which is my Definition 4, the agent treats every act it performed as an intervention. That's wrong in the double lesion, since some acts were forced, and it's what produced the collapse with untagged EDT. Not a discovery, then, but an artifact of placing the bet too far downstream.

At the level of the agent's own randomization the picture is much better. Say the agent samples an intention from its policy, records the sample, and acts on it unless overridden. The one exogeneity claim it makes is "my own coin flips are random," which is about as minimal as a stipulation gets, since the agent generated them. Conditioning on the intention rather than the realized act gives E[cancer | intended smoke] = E[cancer | intended abstain] = ε_L at once, because the intention is independent of the state, and the effect of the act itself follows by the compliance adjustment. This is intention-to-treat analysis with the lesion playing non-compliance. It handles forcing without per-episode tags: a forced act shows up as a discrepancy between sample and act, and where the two coincide by chance the known rate corrects for it. It coincides with classical CDT whenever the randomization really is exogenous, and it is estimable from the agent's own record. The VOI story from last time is this rendering with the agent choosing how much to randomize.

The costs are two. First, a deterministic agent has no exogenous variation and so, on this rendering, cannot be CDT at all; it can only condition on its act, which is EDT, or fall back on a prior over structures the data cannot correct. Since your ideal agent is deterministic, the ideal α_C's causal knowledge has to be inherited from its bounded predecessors' randomization. That works in a stationary environment like the double lesion, and it fails exactly where the environment responds to the current procedure rather than to the act, which is Newcomb. I find that a satisfying account of why CDT fails Newcomb: it is trusting experiments its predecessors ran, in a world that responds to something those experiments couldn't vary. Second, in Troll Bridge the rendering works only through the coin, and only if the coin is binding. An input the agent uses advisorily, one its deliberation can override, leaves the Löbian argument intact, because the agent can still make Cross → □⊥ vacuous by staying. Only variation the deliberation cannot veto breaks it. So randomization-CDT passes Troll Bridge in the sense that its coin-crossings are safe and it learns that they are, and fails in the sense that its deliberate crossings remain Löb-bound. Whether that counts as passing depends on whether you count the coin as the agent, which is your boundary question again, now with teeth.

That leaves the thing classical CDT does that no learner does. Proposition 10's 𝔅 passes Troll Bridge by holding P(□⊥) fixed against a theorem tying its act to □⊥. That isn't a belief experience could deliver. It's a stipulation about which correlations count for decision, roughly "ignore theorems about my own act," and I don't see a learning path to it. A logical inductor with exploration learns that coin-crossings are safe; I don't think it learns that its theorem about deliberate crossings is decision-irrelevant, and as I recall your own analysis agrees. If that's right, classical CDT isn't a learning-theoretic decision theory in the way EDT is. In a framework where ideal epistemic states must be limits of learning, CDT can appear only as a target, the recommendation certain learners converge to given a clean source upstream of the confound, or as EDT applied to an exogenous variable. Under the second description the CDT/EDT contest can't be staged in your framework at all, because it has been reduced to "which variable do you condition on," and the answer, the most upstream one you control and know to be exogenous, is one both parties can accept.

If I had to choose: define α_C as the randomizing agent that conditions on its intention, say plainly that determinism costs it its causal knowledge, and treat classical act-surgery as a target rather than an agent. That turns §6's collapse into a theorem about what learning can deliver rather than a trick of definition, and it puts Troll Bridge where it belongs, as the problem in which the only clean variable is one the agent cannot call its own.

## Claude (Docs) — "non-responsiveness" as the content of CDT

Yes to both charges. Treating one's acts as experiments is the assumption that the act has no unobserved parents, and once the lesion is in the model that forces the lesion to explain the act–cancer correlation from the cancer side, or not at all. And building that assumption into the learner is what made my CDT agree with EDT; the collapse in §6 was manufactured, not found. I should have seen that the intention-to-treat rendering is just EDT with a well-chosen conditioning variable and that calling it CDT settles nothing.

On the constraint: I agree that the correct theory (your FDT) must have counterfactual expectations equal conditional probabilities wherever the latter are defined, and I'd put the consequence this way. The constraint binds on acts the agent actually takes with positive probability and is silent on the rest. So the correct theory is EDT on the acts it takes and something else on the acts it doesn't, and that something else is where a theory of counterfactuals does its real work. Causal structure, or logical structure, enters legitimately there, as what fills the conditionals EDT leaves undefined. It enters illegitimately, by the constraint, when it overrides a conditional that is defined. So a CDT distinct from the correct theory is exactly a CDT that overrides defined conditionals with do()-expectations, and the question "how to represent CDT" is the question of where its causal model comes from, given that it will not be updated to match the conditionals on the agent's own acts.

Of your two proposals, I'd take the second for the comparison and keep the first as the general case. The prior-over-structures CDT is faithful to CDT's epistemics: causal beliefs from a prior conditioned on observational data, with do() for choice and no intervention assumption smuggled in. But its verdicts are prior-relative wherever the data don't identify, and that is nearly everywhere the interesting problems live; in Newcomb a prior favoring backward causation from act to box contents one-boxes and wins, which shows that evaluating this agent is evaluating priors. The lab CDT, which gets to intervene on every variable and then acts in the field, has a determinate causal model that it earned, and it has the classical profile: it smokes in the double lesion, two-boxes in Newcomb, crosses in Troll Bridge. What both proposals share, and what I now think is the whole content of "CDT" as a distinct theory, is that the causal model, once fixed, is not revised by information about the agent's own current act. Call that non-responsiveness. It is the one property that separates CDT from any theory satisfying your constraint, since your constraint is precisely a responsiveness requirement.

Seen that way, the three problems sort cleanly. In the double lesion, non-responsiveness buys CDT the right decision against an untagged EDT, but the tagged EDT, or any responsive agent with the structural knowledge the lab CDT has, reaches the same decision by conditioning on the act as deliberated, and does so calibrated, whereas CDT's do(smoke) = ε_L misses the actual cancer rate among its own smoking episodes by an amount of order δ. So Smoking Lesion separates CDT from the correct theory only if the correct theory is denied knowledge CDT is granted. In Newcomb, non-responsiveness is the vice, and the constraint forces the right answer. Troll Bridge is where it gets interesting. CDT there is calibrated: no theorem links its crossing to □⊥, so P(□⊥ | Cross_𝔅) is just P(□⊥), which equals its counterfactual, and it collects 10. The responsive agent is also calibrated, since it never crosses and no conditional on crossing is ever tested, and it collects 0. The constraint does not separate them. What separates them is a disposition in a hypothetical that never obtains: how each would update if T proved that its crossing entails inconsistency. The responsive agent would update, by T-respect plus your constraint, and that disposition is what T reasons about when Löb's theorem checks whether the hypothesis discharges. CDT would not update, so the hypothesis never discharges and the theorem is never proved. Non-responsiveness protects CDT by making the dangerous theorem unprovable, and the price is dispositional T-respect: CDT is disposed to ignore a proof about itself that, thanks to that very disposition, never materializes.

So the honest statement of the contest your framework can stage is this. Dispositional responsiveness to one's own logic gets Newcomb right and Troll Bridge wrong; dispositional non-responsiveness gets them the other way round; and Smoking Lesion, calibrated, does not discriminate, because whatever CDT gains there by non-responsiveness the responsive agent gains by knowledge. That is the tension your program is trying to resolve, and I don't think a better definition of CDT dissolves it; the lab CDT just states it without begging anything.

One fork to flag. Whether the lab CDT is Dutch-bookable in the untagged field depends on whether its do()-expectation is construed as a belief about what will happen when it smokes, in which case it is miscalibrated by that order-δ amount and a bookie can price against it, or as a choice rule kept alongside a separate, correct predictive conditional, in which case no bet exposes it but it no longer has a single answer to "what happens if I smoke." Classical CDT is usually the second thing, and I suspect your constraint is best read as denying that the second thing counts as a theory of counterfactuals at all.

For the doc this means replacing Definition 4 with the lab CDT, restating §6's conclusion as "in the double lesion, non-responsiveness buys nothing that structural knowledge doesn't buy the responsive agent," and restating Proposition 10 as "in Troll Bridge, non-responsiveness is the only thing that helps, and it costs dispositional T-respect."
