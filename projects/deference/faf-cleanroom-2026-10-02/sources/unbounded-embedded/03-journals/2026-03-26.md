			- `a decision-theoretic approach for managing misalignment`
				- Probability frames treat the principal's beliefs as given, and the agent's beliefs as uncertain. The probability space is assumed to be finite, meaning the agent's beliefs have been narrowed down to a finite set of possibilities. I suppose this assumption is probably not too critical? I worry a bit that there is a 'size problem' hidden here; the principle and the agent are being modeled in an asymmetric way. Is the model missing something about them recursively modeling each other? I'm not sure.
				- The model certainly doesn't account for the principal or agent recursively modeling themselves; eg, it doesn't deal with paradoxical sentences such as "my credence in this sentence is < 1/2". Is this important to the phenomena studied by the paper? [Dorst](https://www.kevindorst.com/research.html) thinks our uncertainty about our beliefs changes the picture of rationality significantly.
					- Generally speaking, I do feel strongly that representing beliefs as a random variable (avoiding explicit beliefs-about-beliefs embedded into the sigma algebra itself) misses something important about the structure of beliefs-about-beliefs; it essentially sets aside logic, caring only for probability. The same modeling attitude deals with computational uncertainty by steering away from sigma-algebras that embed PA within them; computable probability distributions are possible if we restrict the language enough, obscuring the idea that realistic agents must be probabilistically inconsistent because it is not realistic to know all the consequences of your own beliefs. I think the Diagonal Lemma reveals something important about the structure of human concepts, and perhaps concepts more generally; cognitively rich minds will satisfy it, and ruling it out for the sake of simplicity does seem to risk missing important structure. Thus, if (cognitively rich) agents can reason probabilistically about their own beliefs, they'll have to contend with troublesome self-referential cases somehow -- trying to avoid this is a fool's errand.
					- More concretely, these details are certainly impactful for the Reflection Principle; the principle becomes untenable in the extreme, as noted in the LI paper. It must be relaxed (or other principles must be relaxed to keep it consistent), meaning that a version of the result cited here would have to become quantitative rather than absolute.
				- We could model `value uncertainty` by treating U as a luv, that is, a real-valued variable in the domain of discourse (represented in the event algebra) -- thus making value uncertainty epistemic. However, it would then be absurd to consider possible decisions in the way it is being done here (in the pure epistemic uncertainty section). Instead, decisions could be between events. This theory would probably have a similar character to the one developed, I suppose.

			- `Margins of Misalignment`
				- 
		- can we characterize a space of 'updates' that are `open-minded UDT`-like, and hence, trustworthy?
			- UDT can trust itself, and of course updateful dt can trust itself in a narrower set of circumstances, but UDT can also trust future selves with different priors to some extent, which is not bayes-update in general.
				- Correlating with the truth, but keeping spread of belief over facts that would be dangerous to learn -- something along the lines of back up to a prior roughly as ignorant as omega's?
				- The point is, decision deference must require more than pure epistemic endorsement. There are situations where epistemically endorsed updates cannot be decision-endorsed.
		- The definition of modesty seems off. The definition used is "the expert doesn't know exactly what their probabilities are", but this is glossed as "the expert isn't certain they're worthy of deference". I get the vibe it is being interpreted like "not sure you're the expert" as if full self-knowledge implies you're the expert.
			- In the notation of the paper, $P$ is a definite description for the expert's opinions, whatever they are. $\mathcal{P}_w$ is a rigid designator for the expert's opinions at world $w$. They define **modesty**: there exist some $q,t$ such that $P(q)=t$ but $P\big( P(q)=t \big)<1$.
				- 
			- To my mind, a more sensible definition of modesty would be that there exists some $q,t$ such that $P(q)=t$ and $P\big(q | P(q)=t \big) \neq t$; ie, the expert would not defer to its own opinions. This _implies_ the notion of modesty used in the paper, but is not implied by it.
				- I don't think this proposal is a uniquely good notion of modesty; there are other relevant ways beliefs can be modest. For example, the expert might think you should not defer to their opinion.
		- **Reflection**: The standard theory of deference, in the paper's narrative, is based on the `reflection principle` (which the authors sensibly regard as a family of related principles).
			- The problem with this account, as they see it, is that it is incompatible with imperfect self-knowledge on the part of the expert.
			- I'm tempted to lean on the `logical induction` analysis.
				- Complete self-knowledge (immodesty, in the paper's parlance) leads to paradox, due to self-referential sentences such as "this sentence has probability less than x". We can only demand that the self-knowledge be very precise, accurate to within some small tolerance.
					- This argument depends on the diagonal lemma, and for the claims about probabilities of sentences to be predicates. Similar assumptions will also imply that the probability distribution cannot be computed. Imperfect probabilistic coherence must also therefore be tolerated.
				- Relatedly, we can only demand that reflection-type trust properties hold to within some tolerance, also; there must be room to doubt "the expert assigns probability less than 0.4 to this sentence" when you learn the expert believes it.
				- What happens to the theory when we consider these relaxations? 
					- Do quantitative limitations on self-knowledge imply quantitative limitations on epistemic `endorsement`? 
					- How does quantitative epistemic endorsement relate to quantitative decision endorsement?
