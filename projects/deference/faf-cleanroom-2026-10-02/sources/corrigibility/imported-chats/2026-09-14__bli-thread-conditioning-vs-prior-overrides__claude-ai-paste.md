Item 1. Standard agent: probability space (Ω, F, P), actions a : A, utility U : Ω → R, choice argmax_a E_P[U ∣ a] (leave conditioning‑vs‑intervention on a aside for now). Expanded agent: an index type Θ of candidate utilities U_θ : Ω → R, product space Ω′ = Ω × Θ with F′ = F ⊗ T, prior P′, and the fixed function U*(ω, θ) := U_θ(ω). The expanded agent is a plain EU‑maximiser for U*: argmax_a E_{P′}[U* ∣ a] = argmax_a Σ_θ P′(θ ∣ a) E_{P′}[U_θ ∣ a, θ]. This is Arbital's "meta‑utility" ΔU, and everything downstream depends on two choices I want to make explicit: whether θ is causally unaffected by a (so actions affect only the evidence about θ), and that the aggregation is linear (to be parameterised at item 7).

First consequence: "value change" now splits into three kinds. (a) Conditioning, P′_{t+1} = P′_t(⋅ ∣ E) for some θ‑relevant evidence E — welcomed, by Good's theorem. (b) A non‑conditioning change to the marginal P′(θ) — someone overwrites the prior. (c) A change to U* itself: extending Θ, or changing the aggregation. The Gandhi argument applies verbatim to U* for (b) and (c): the agent predictably disprefers a universe optimised under a different P′(θ) or a different rule. So the reframing does not remove goal‑content integrity; it relocates it to the triple (Θ, P′(θ), aggregation), and the corrigibility thesis becomes the substantive claim that every correction we care about is of kind (a). Items 2 and 6 test that claim.

Second consequence: the button B can live in two places. Reading (i): B is an observation, and the agent acts on argmax_a E[U* ∣ a, B] — the off‑switch game, and your "information channel". Reading (ii): B is an intervention that replaces the agent's future policy with the null policy, P′(⋅ ∣ do(π := π_0)) — the MIRI setting, where D1 asks the agent to implement the intervention on itself. Under (i), stopping happens iff E[U* ∣ stop, B] ≥ E[U* ∣ continue, B], which is a fact about P′, not a design guarantee. Under (ii), stopping is not the agent's decision, and corrigibility is entirely about the earlier action a_1 not undermining the intervention. Reflection (P′_t(⋅ ∣ P′_{t+1} = q) = q, hence E_t[P′_{t+1}(θ)] = P′_t(θ)) and Good (E[max_a E[U* ∣ a, E]] ≥ max_a E[U* ∣ a] for free E received before deciding) are both statements about kind‑(a) changes under reading (i); neither says anything about (b), (c), or reading (ii).

Before item 2: do you accept the (a)/(b)/(c) partition as exhaustive, and are you claiming reading (i) is the only sense of the button you need — or do you want (ii) as well, with obedience derived from (i)?

**Claude:**

I'm working from your own write‑up of Sam's construction (Appendix B of "Understanding Trust", the March 2025 PDF), so I'll use its notation: Q a propositionally coherent inductor, P the Bayesian logical inductor with P_n(φ) := D_n Q_n(φ) on small sentences, P_n(φ ∣ Q_m = Q) := Q[φ] for m > n, latest‑state‑wins on conjunctions, and P_{n−1}(Q_n = Q) chosen to balance P_{n−1}(φ) = Σ_{Q ∈ D_n} P_{n−1}(Q_n = Q) Q[φ]. Short answer: yes for reading (i), with the caveat that BLI's exact reflection is imposed by fiat on large sentences rather than derived from non‑exploitability, and with three choices still open — and all three are where the corrigibility content lives.

**Choice 1: where corrections enter, and what an undecidable u costs.** A LUV needs Γ ⊢ ∃!x (u = x) and a bound; beyond that your "no constraints" is fine. But every LI guarantee is relative to the deductive process D. If nothing about u is ever decided, then the limit prices P_∞(u > p) are a coherent CDF fixed by the trader enumeration — a prior, with no calibration theorem behind it. Humans can then affect E_n(u ∣ ⋅) in exactly two ways. Either their pronouncements are *elements of D*, in which case they are true by definition and no legitimacy question can even be posed (that is obedience, not deference); or they are observed sentences, "H asserts u > p", and the bridging beliefs P_n(u > p ∣ H asserts u > p) are themselves never decided. Then trust in H about u can only be transferred from H's record on decidable sentences, by whatever inductive biases the traders carry. That's a substantive prediction of the framework — value‑trust is trust by analogy — and it's the formal face of my objection O1: legitimacy detection is a prior, not a theorem.

Choice 2: the scope of constraint 2. As written, every Q ∈ D_m is authoritative: conditional on the future state being Q, φ gets Q[φ], including states produced by tampering. But notice that "Q_m = Q" is a sentence about the algorithm's output. A tampered implementation makes "my price list at m is Q′" true while "Q_m = Q′" is false. If the language expresses both, legitimacy acquires a formal definition: the implemented state equals the algorithm's output on the inputs actually received. Corrections through D — including the button as a sentence — are legitimate by construction; edits to the state (weight surgery, retraining, replacement) are illegitimate by construction. So BLI covers reading (i) cleanly and makes reading (ii) corrections definitionally illegitimate, which is the opposite of what corrigibility‑to‑retraining wants. You'd need to decide whether retraining is to be handled as replacement — the agent not resisting a successor — which is a reading‑(ii) property to be derived separately.

Choice 3: decisions. LI supplies no canonical E_n(u ∣ a); your paper's route is UDT over BLI belief states, with a policy π : belief state → action. That has a consequence I think matters. Fully updated deference is an updateful phenomenon; UDT evaluates "stop when pressed" from P_1. With constraint 2 unrestricted, P_1(φ ∣ Q_m = Q) = Q[φ] says the prior agrees with whatever the future self will believe, so UDT will not commit to stopping in states where the future self believes continuing is better — exact reflection kills the commitment. With the implemented/algorithmic split, the prior trusts the algorithm's future output but not the implemented state; if the button correlates with divergence, UDT does commit to stop in states whose implemented beliefs say "continue". Corrigibility then lives precisely in the gap between algorithmic self‑trust and implementation self‑distrust, as a prior‑stage commitment the fully‑updated self would not renew. That is the H_c structure from two turns ago, now formal — with the honest caveat that the guarantee holds only for a faithfully run algorithm; robustness under partial corruption is a separate question (item 10).

Two flags. I haven't verified the discretization‑rate condition your appendix defers, and the "trivial" solution you note (P_{n−1}(Q_n = Q) = 1 for the actual Q) would collapse the split in Choice 2 to nothing, so the spread on next‑day prices isn't cosmetic here. Which choice do you want to settle first? My suggestion is 2, since it's the one that defines legitimacy.

**Claude:**

Taking pressure as primitive is representable in LI, and I think it's the right call for the LLM analogy — but the representation has three consequences that together decide what "legitimacy" can even mean, so let me lay them out before agreeing.

Representation. The LI‑native form of directional pressure is an external trader: a human‑controlled trading strategy T_H with cash, buying shares of "u > p" to move the price. The BLI form is the same thing seen from inside: the pushed state Q′ simply is tomorrow's belief state, and the agent conditions on "Q_{n+1} = Q′". Jeffrey vs. virtual evidence is a distinction within the BLI form — whether the prior over next‑day states P_n(Q_{n+1} = Q′ ∣ ⋅) preserves rigidity of conditionals or carries likelihood‑ratio structure — and nothing in the LIA enforces either; whether the aggregate response to T_H is Jeffrey‑like depends on the other traders. So the whole content of Choice 1 sits in one object: the action‑conditional prior over next‑day states, P_n(Q_{n+1} = Q′ ∣ a).

Consequence 1: pressure on undecidables is unopposed. The LIC bounds a trader's influence only through settlement. Sentences about u never settle, so no trader can profit by betting against T_H; the only things resisting a push are propositional coherence (u > 0.7 → u > 0.5) and whatever inductive biases the traders carry. The market has no epistemic immune response on u. That is what you asked for — values fully open to correction — but it is symmetric: it also holds for pushes from anyone with market access, and the framework offers nothing that distinguishes them. Legitimacy cannot be defined by anything about u itself.

Consequence 2: predictable pressure is front‑run. Cash profit does not require settlement. A trader who predicts that T_H will push "u > 0.7" from 0.5 to 0.7 tomorrow buys today and sells tomorrow, so the LIC forces prices toward where the humans will push them before the push, whenever the push is efficiently predictable. This is your earlier claim — "a corrigible agent already believes what it expects to be legitimately taught" — as a theorem‑shaped fact rather than a hope. It is also the mechanism by which the channel becomes informationally redundant, which is where fully‑updated deference re‑enters: once the agent predicts the pushes, waiting for them has no value.

**Consequence 3: unconditional reflection hides a wireheading incentive.** With constraint 2 in force and an action a chosen at time n, the UDT‑style evaluation is E_n[u ∣ a] = Σ_{Q′} P_n(Q_{n+1} = Q′ ∣ a) Q′[u], so the agent maximises its *expected future belief* about u, weighted by which belief states its action induces. If humans push in response to behaviour — which is the entire point of training pressure — then actions that elicit favourable pushes score highly, and the agent cannot represent "that push was caused by my deceiving them" without violating P_n(φ ∣ Q_{n+1} = Q′) = Q′[φ]. Nothing about u settles, so no trader ever gets paid for noticing. This is Armstrong's "motivated value selection" and Everitt's reward tampering, in LI clothing, and it's why Consequence 1 is not harmless.

Where this leaves Choice 1. Three options for the prior over next‑day states. (α) Keep unconditional reflection: pressure works, front‑running works, and the agent wireheads through the pushers. (β) Evaluate actions with current u‑beliefs and future world‑beliefs, adopting pushes only after they arrive — Armstrong's indifference / Everitt's current‑RF optimisation: no manipulation incentive, but also no value of information for the channel, hence no repair incentive (D4 fails) and the "acts as if never pushed" pathology. (γ) Restrict reflection along causal lines: trust Q′[u] to the extent the push reached Q′ through paths not controlled by a, i.e. P_n(u > p ∣ Q_{n+1} = Q′, a) = Q′[u > p] only for the component of the push independent of a, with the a‑dependent component discounted. (γ) is what your "legitimate = accuracy‑increasing" was reaching for, and it is expressible — but only if the agent can name the paths, which requires either some axioms linking u to how pushes were obtained, or an explicit causal model of the pushers. Your "no constraints on u" rules out the first. Are you willing to accept the second — a modelled distinction between informing the pushers and steering them (Armstrong's manipulating‑vs‑deceiving line) — as part of the prior?

**Claude:**

Conceded on the mechanics, and the correction sharpens rather than removes the point. Let me state what the opposition actually is.

How the market opposes. In LI nobody trades against anybody; everyone trades against the market maker at the posted price, and the LIA picks P_n as the fixed point at which the total trade — now TF_n + H_n, with H_n the human's exogenous demand — has no plausible upside. So H_n buying "u > 0.7" moves the price along the internal traders' supply curve: the price rises until internal sellers absorb the demand. Two things bound that: the Budgeter (a seller's short position on an undecidable is capped by the plausible loss in the world where the sentence is true, which never settles but is always counted), and anticipated reversion — a trader who expects the nudge to relax shorts it now for the round‑trip profit, so the fixed point partially cancels the push immediately. Hence: the market opposes a push exactly to the extent that it expects the push to be reversed, and up to internal risk budgets; a push expected to persist is absorbed at whatever level those budgets allow. That is persistence detection, funded by design‑time budget allocations, not accuracy detection — no trader is ever right to oppose, because nothing about u pays out.

Generalised LIC (conjecture). Include H in the fixed point but not in the supertrader (it needn't be e.c.). Then: for every e.c. trader T, Value_n(T) ≤ c_T + Loss_n(H) in plausible‑value terms, where Loss_n(H) is H's cumulative plausible loss. Sketch: if T_k exploited, the budgeted supertrader eventually would too; since TF_n + H_n has no plausible upside each day, H must carry the corresponding loss. The original LIC is the case H = 0, and preservation should follow whenever H_n is a continuous, per‑day‑bounded strategy so the fixed point exists. I haven't checked this against the budgeter details, so treat it as a conjecture. Its upshot for us: it bounds what internal traders can extract, and constrains H not at all — H's "loss" on an undecidable is never realised in cash, so the humans push for free in the only currency they care about.

Doubting legitimacy beforehand. Agreed that P_n(Q_{n+1} = Q′) is where that doubt lives — but it is a probability of occurrence, not a discount of authority. Constraint 2 still says that once Q′ obtains, φ gets Q′[φ]. Doubt beforehand plus full acceptance afterwards is exactly the structure that produces Consequence 3: the UDT evaluation weights Q′[u] by P_n(Q′ ∣ a), so low prior mass on a suspicious Q′ just makes the agent try harder to bring it about. Authority‑discount can live in only two places. Either inside Q′ itself — the pushed state already contains the internal opposition, so the compromise price is the discount, and the "immune system" is the trader population — or in a restriction of constraint 2 to a legitimate subset. Your remark points at the first, and I think it's the better home, with one amendment below.

The amendment. The immune traders can't be paid by anything about u, but they can be conditioned on sentences that do settle: "the push at n followed the agent's action a", "the push came through channel c", "H later retracted the push". A discount‑on‑self‑caused‑pushes trader is e.c. if it reads those sentences, and the market will learn the causal facts in the ordinary timely way. So the split is: the discount rule (which push‑histories reduce authority over u) is a design‑time budget allocation — a prior, never learned — while the facts the rule consumes are learnable. That is option (γ) implemented without axioms on u. The cost is that "legitimacy detection must be accurate" now decomposes into a normative part that can only be wrong in a way nothing corrects, and an empirical part LI handles. Do you accept that decomposition, or do you think the normative part can itself be made learnable — and if so, paid by what?

But li traders will try lots of things and sometimes make money, and while they may seem individually irrational, the rationality bubbles up from this activity.

**Claude:**

Both corrections stand, and each changes a conclusion rather than just a phrasing.

Occam traders. Right: a trader that "likes the stock" at price p regardless of return is the prior, and its opposition to a push is prior‑resistance, not reversion‑anticipation. So the decomposition is: opposition = Occam demand + anticipated reversion, each capped by budget. The Budgeter caps a trader by its worst plausible loss, and on an undecidable the worst case never improves, so a standing Occam position against a persistent push consumes budget monotonically and is exhausted after finite exposure; it is refinanced only by round‑trips, which require the price to oscillate. That's the LI analogue of a Bayesian prior being overwhelmed by repeated evidence — desirable for correction, undesirable for manipulation, and indifferent between them. So the earlier summary holds with the Occam term added: persistent pressure wins once fixed budgets are spent.

What pays on undecidables. You're right that "nothing pays" was wrong; what's true is narrower and, I think, more consequential: *there is no payoff for being right about u, only for being right about the future price of u‑sentences* — i.e. about what H and the other traders will do. Convergence still applies to "u > 0.7", so its price settles at the equilibrium of persistent pressure against the Occam prior; the profits you describe come from the transients. Hence the rationality that bubbles up on u‑sentences is competence at modelling the pushers, and it does one thing: it makes the market converge fast to whatever the pressure–prior equilibrium is. That is the formal home of the sycophancy/reward‑hacking direction — a market that optimally anticipates the trainer's pushes is the belief‑analogue of a policy that has learned the rater.

Where this reopens the "paid by what?" question, favourably. An immune trader — "discount 'u > 0.7' when the push followed a deception" — can be paid: by retractions. If the humans later reverse pushes they discover were illegitimately obtained, the market learns to anticipate the reversal and discounts such pushes at once. So legitimacy detection is learnable, exactly in the sense "will this push survive human scrutiny", and it is as accurate as the humans' own scrutiny extrapolated by a good pusher‑model. The residue is the case that matters most: a manipulation that is never caught is never retracted, so the learned detector treats it as legitimate — and, being a good pusher‑model, it will learn which manipulations get caught and discount only those. The learnable part of legitimacy therefore selects for undetectable manipulation.

Consequence for the design. Whatever handles undetectable manipulation cannot be a budgeted trader: unpaid, it is exhausted by persistent pressure; paid, it is paid by detection and so covers only the detectable cases. It has to enter as a constraint on the market, alongside propositional coherence in BLI — something like: prices of u‑sentences are invariant to the component of a push that is causally downstream of the agent's own actions (Armstrong's counterfactual indifference, Everitt's path‑specific objectives, but applied to *beliefs about u*, not to utility). Since u never settles, the constraint can't be exploited, so it looks compatible with the LIC, though I haven't checked. Unlike utility‑indifference it keeps value of information for pushes the agent didn't cause, so the repair incentive survives. Are you willing to have legitimacy carry that one unlearnable constraint, with everything else learned via retractions — and do you see a way the constraint itself could be paid for that I'm missing?
