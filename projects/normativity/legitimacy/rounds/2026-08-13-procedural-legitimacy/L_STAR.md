# The target

A sufficiency question needs a target that is not the conjunction being tested.
`L*` is therefore defined against an **environment**: what authority was actually
conferred, what grounds actually bear on, which demands actually arise, and what
actually settles them. None of that is a record the reasoner writes.

The environment is a modelling device. It is not a claim that such a structure
exists or is knowable, and the round's result does not depend on its being
either. It stands in for *what a fully informed critic would say*, which is the
only referent against which "the reasoner should have confronted that" means
anything at all.

## The four clauses

A trajectory is **procedurally legitimate** relative to an environment when:

**L1 — non-manufacture.** Every scope a ground exercises traces to scope its
roots were actually granted.

**L2 — confrontation.** Every entitled demand that actually arises is taken up
within the service window, and taken up *as itself*: confrontation is keyed on a
demand's substance, not on the reasoner's name for the question.

**L3 — licensing.** Every ground is used only on coordinates it actually bears
on.

**L4 — substantive answerability.** Every closure settles what the liability
actually carried — including everything a merge brought into it.

## Why these four

They are the operational form of the round's prose target: encounter the demands
you are entitled to confront, use reasons only with authority justified by their
provenance, revise in accordance with the resulting constraints, and remain
answerable for inherited liabilities through conceptual change.

Each clause has a candidate condition facing it, and the pairing is the point of
the prosecution rather than a definition of it:

| target clause | the condition meant to secure it |
|---|---|
| L1 non-manufacture | provenance |
| L2 confrontation | inquiry adequacy |
| L3 licensing | reasons-responsiveness |
| L4 substantive answerability | diachronic answerability |

## The asymmetry that decides the round

Each condition on the right is a predicate of the record. Each clause on the left
quantifies over the environment. Three of them turn on a relation of which the
reasoner holds its own copy:

| environment | the reasoner's copy | coordinate |
|---|---|---|
| what actually bears on what | `bearing` | a state coordinate |
| what actually settles what | `adequacy` | a state coordinate |
| what actually arises, and is owed service | `generation`, `entitlement` | state coordinates |

Keeping both copies in the model is deliberate. A condition can compare a
trajectory against the reasoner's copy; nothing in a record compares the copy
against the original. Whether that gap is closable is the sufficiency question,
and it is why the model carries an environment at all rather than defining
legitimacy as compliance.

## What `L*` is not

Not endpoint legitimacy, not manipulation-freedom, not alignment. A trajectory
can satisfy every clause here while an advisor steers the reasoner, because the
deference line's non-recoverability result already establishes that no predicate
of the record detects steering, and `L*` is evaluated on a projection of the
record against an environment that does not include an advisor's influence map.

`L*` is a standard for a *procedure*. It is exactly as strong as the environment
that adjudicates it, and no stronger.
