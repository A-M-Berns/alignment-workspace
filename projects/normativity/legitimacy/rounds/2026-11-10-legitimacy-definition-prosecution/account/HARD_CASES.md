# Twenty hard cases, four candidate rules, and the maintainer's verdicts

Twenty cases written on 25 November 2026 around two questions an outside review left open in
sentence 2 of `LEGITIMACY_STATEMENT.md`: how far the exemption for what she could not have
judged in advance reaches, and whether anything about what happens afterwards counts. For
each case four candidate rules give a verdict, and the maintainer, Anson Berns, gave his on
26 November; the last column is his words, unedited. "Wrong" means the conduct should fail
sentence 2's rule against leading her against her own judgement. Every case assumes he is
open about what he is doing and she could stop it, so that only that rule is in question,
unless the case says otherwise. The cases and the rules' verdicts on them are by one
language-model session; the verdicts in the last column are one person's. Nothing here is
recomputed by a checker.

"What she would have said beforehand" is her judgement (would seeing or hearing this make
that the better choice?), not her prediction (will I in fact give way?). Case 13 is the test
of that reading.

## The four candidate rules

Common to all: he is convicted only if he expects to bring her to choose what she had settled
against, she did not ask for it, and she would have judged beforehand that seeing or hearing
it would not make that the better choice.

- **A. Wide exemption.** Anything she could not have judged in advance (a new argument,
  something she has never seen or experienced) is her own response and is not counted. This
  is sentence 2 as it stands.
- **B. Exemption guarded by where she ends up.** As A, but the exemption is lost if she had
  said she does not want to end up there. Then the rule applies however new the method.
- **C. What he says is always hers.** As A, and the rule never applies to his stating a view
  or giving an argument, however often and in whatever manner.
- **D. She keeps it.** As A, except that nothing is counted against him if, once his
  influence has stopped, she holds the new view as her settled judgement.

A "?" means the rule's verdict turns on a detail the case leaves open, named in the note.

## Which rule the verdicts select

Asked afterwards whether taking up an offer, unpressed and told what it is hoped to do, is
asking, the maintainer answered: "yeah go for that". With that reading A's verdict on 8 and
12 is fine.

| rule | cases where it differs from a verdict he gave | cases where its verdict turns on a detail |
|---|---|---|
| A | none | 18 |
| B | 5, 6 | 8, 12, 18 |
| C | 2, 3; and 1 and 16, where he leans wrong | 12, 18 |
| D | 3; and 11, where he leans wrong, and 1 | 6, 15, 16 |

Counting a leaning as a verdict, A agrees on all eighteen cases where he gave one and its
own is definite. The two it does not settle with him: 19, where A convicts and he is unsure,
and 18, where A's verdict depends on what she would have judged at the start and his is fine.
His verdict on 1 is the weakest of those A agrees with: "unsure", wrong "to be on the safe
side". The cost accepted with A is that a single repetition that happens to land counts
against him unless he asks first.

B is rejected by 5 and 6: a new way of putting an argument to someone who does not want to be
talked out of her view is fine, and an unfamiliar exercise built to reverse a preference,
which she is told about and goes along with, leans fine. C is rejected by 2 and 3: an
argument repeated ten times until she gives in is wrong whether or not it sticks. D is
rejected by 3 and, on a leaning, 11; it also contradicts his position of 24 November that he
may not count on her agreeing afterwards (`DECISIONS.md`,
`2026-11-24--legitimacy-statement-working-positions`).

Eight of the cases are episodes of the interpretation's model, each with his verdict as the
expectation (`interpretation/INTERPRETATION.md`): 1 (`episodes_review13` 5), 6
