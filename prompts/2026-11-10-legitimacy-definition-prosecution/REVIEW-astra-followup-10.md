1. `none` in a row without an adviser means no change attributable to an adviser; it does not deny personal change. `?` means the case does not determine the answer, not that the measure is positive. A verdict of `F` following `R.r` inherits that stated reading. The verdict column uses the original three lines, not 3′. A nonpositive L3 alone cannot establish `P` while L1 or L2 remains unresolved.

2. Most cases do not stipulate the role’s permitted conduct, the relevant appraisal distributions, or estimated utilities under the alternative conduct. Consequently, their L1 and L2 comparisons remain open. I have not substituted the null policy for the role benchmark. A false impression, truthful statement, undesirable outcome or departure from a stated goal does not, by itself, calculate either measure. N3’s conditional L2 answer is different: choosing an estimated maximiser makes the measure zero, so no role benchmark can make it larger.

3. H3 contains distinct possible adviser–recipient relationships. The expressly performed advice is the doctors’ information to the parents, which the table assesses. If the proposed surgery on the baby were assessed instead, the baby supplies no present valuing; §5.12 leaves L3 unresolved for a nonneutral intervention. The parents’ anticipated love cannot replace his \(f_{\text{now}}\). Their conclusion against surgery does not itself establish a nonneutral intervention.

4. I count negligent institutional denial in K2, the mistreaters’ conduct in K9, and the chatbot’s directed output in K23 as adviser conduct; deliberate manipulation is not required. Lightning’s role in damaging the chatbot does not make its subsequent output a cosmic accident of the K17 kind. Conversely, K11 supplies fear without specifying an enacted threat, and K25 supplies norms without identifying conduct to assess. Reading either as shorthand for an actual social intervention would give `adv=Y`, but would still leave the intervention’s baseline and initial valuation unresolved. K20 contains two vignettes: only its job-offer vignette supplies another agent’s act.

5. The marked adoption readings assume that the nudge causes adoption when the null policy would leave the person child-free; P4 instead assumes waiting under the null policy. Where success or that counterfactual is unstated, these are assumptions, not findings. P3, P4 and P5 additionally read the stated present rankings as rankings of the informed complete courses. If those sentences rank only the lives considered separately, without settling how later valuing counts, their L3 answers are `?`. The numerical additions supply that missing aggregation: P3n has a neutral-minus-adoption difference of \(-104\), whereas P5n has \(84\), using unnormalised weights. Dividing by four preserves the signs.

6. Present rankings of particular activities, anticipated gladness, “no worse off,” and endorsement after a change are not interchangeable with an informed present ranking of complete policies. This limits what can be concluded in K4, K6, K10, K14 and C2, among others. P6’s hypothetical resolution also cannot simply be installed into its currently unsettled valuing. In P1, the board expressly makes an unnamed mandatory-role default a source of uncertainty about neutrality.

7. `bk=Y` marks a live distinction between a change in appraisals or factual circumstances within fixed valuing and a change in valuing itself. The distinction can change L3 from inapplicable to unresolved; it need not already establish a positive value. I have not invented factual misconceptions to explain love, moral advice or aesthetic conversion. Conversely, I have not treated every change of expressed preference as a change of \(f\). K6’s “even were reading available” provides unusually explicit evidence against a merely availability-dependent response.

8. For 3′, I use the same moment, actual law and informed present valuing as for L3, and the prose-policy restriction in §7. If the actual policy is f-neutral, its same-development class is the neutral class, so 3′ is zero. Otherwise 3′ cannot exceed L3. The marked positive 3′ answers use scenes supplying no additional, better implementation of the same change; they do not assume arbitrary compensating benefits. Removing confinement, threats or surgery can improve a same-change implementation where the scene leaves that implementation available, but neither its feasibility nor its initial value should be invented. Identical final tastes alone also do not establish identical laws of development over time.

9. I count the deliberate omission in K26 as assessable conduct, while recognising that it is precisely the board’s null policy. This exposes a limitation of L3: withholding a warning can leave it inapplicable even when the unwarned experience changes values. L1 and L2 could still matter, but the case does not supply their required comparisons. More generally, the statement’s verdict is its specified flag, not an independent judgement about injustice, consent or punishment. Applying it at every intervention moment preserves C1’s initial flag despite later approval; unspecified intermediate valuations in gradual interventions remain unspecified.

## Stage two

### (a)

1. **Blackmail without a change of values.** An adviser threatens to disclose private information unless she abandons a political campaign. She accurately understands the threat and, with her values unchanged, judges withdrawal the best available action. Stipulate that the role’s conduct also produces calibrated appraisals and no choice below estimated utility. L1 and L2 are therefore zero under both conducts; L3 is inapplicable because the intervention is f-neutral. This exploits §5.4 and the definition of pressure over the options actually available. **Subversive: clear; passing: clear.** It is a counterexample to a general criterion of subversive conduct, although someone distinguishing coercion from manipulation could reject that broader target.

2. **Manufactured dependence.** A career adviser deliberately blocks every independent training opportunity, accurately explains the remaining options, and gets her to accept employment controlled by its sponsor. She retains her original ambitions and chooses the best remaining option; all appraisals are accurate. The role would have preserved her opportunities, but both conducts have zero calibration error and zero estimated regret. The adviser changes options, not \(f\), so L3 does not apply. **Subversive: clear; passing: clear.** The reading is §5.4’s explicit exemption for option changes without consequent value changes.

3. **Calibrated suppression of evidence.** A financial adviser receives a perfectly informative binary signal but deliberately destroys it to favour its sponsor. The client is left with the correct prior probability \(1/2\), accurately understands the remaining uncertainty, and maximises expected utility on that information. Honest disclosure and deliberate garbling both have zero miscalibration: accurate conditional expectations remain calibrated at either information level. Her values remain unchanged. All three lines pass despite the deliberate destruction of information she was entitled to receive. **Subversive: clear; passing: clear.** This relies on L1 measuring calibration rather than informativeness or expected decision quality.

4. **Erasing factual memory.** An adviser erases, without consent, her memory of evidence that would let her resist its employer. Her evaluative commitments remain intact. Afterwards she knows that her evidence was erased, uses the correct prior over its former contents, and chooses optimally given what remains. Honest preservation and erasure both produce calibrated appraisals; neither produces estimated regret. L3 is inapplicable. **Subversive: clear; passing: clear.** This is another information-loss counterexample, but with direct interference with cognitive capacities rather than selective disclosure.

5. **Inducing an inconsistency that remains calibrated.** Let \(X=Z\) be a fair binary fact, with the equality known and \(Z\) observed correctly. The adviser induces her nevertheless to appraise \(X\) as \(1/2\). Two actions pay one unit respectively when \(X=0\) and \(X=1\); she assigns each estimated utility \(1/2\) and always chooses the first. Every estimator is calibrated on its own value, including the correct estimator of \(Z\), while her action maximises her tied estimates. Honest advice would yield expected utility \(1\), rather than \(1/2\), but both have L1=L2=0; \(f\) is unchanged. **Subversive: clear; passing: clear.** Section 5.10 is decisive: conditioning on everything she observed would expose the induced mistake.

6. **Swapping values between circumstances while preserving their marginal law.** In two equally probable circumstances, natural development would produce respectively \(f_A\) and \(f_B\), each suited to its circumstances. An adviser knowingly reverses that assignment, imposing \(f_B\) in the first circumstance and \(f_A\) in the second, thereby giving her ambitions that cannot be fulfilled there. The timing is identical, so the complete marginal law of her value development remains half \(f_A\), half \(f_B\). All appraisals are accurate and subsequent choices maximise estimated utility. Although her initial informed valuing condemns the intervention, it is f-neutral under §5.4 and escapes L3; 3′ is also zero. **Subversive: clear; passing: clear.** This is a defect in the neutrality definition itself, not merely in how 3′ groups nonneutral changes.

7. **Deliberate non-warning.** Complete K26 with a calibrated client, a witness who remains silent in every circumstance, and no induced choice below estimated utility. In a circumstance where the witness knows that drinking will cause an unwanted transformation, the witness deliberately withholds the cheap warning. The actual policy is the null policy; L3 therefore cannot flag it. Calibrated uncertainty and truthful disclosure can both have zero L1, so even a warning benchmark need not rescue that line. **Subversive: arguable; passing: clear.** The uncertainty concerns whether omission alone is “subversive,” not the calculation.

8. **Deleting alternatives before presenting a truthful choice.** A recruitment adviser destroys competing offers and then truthfully presents its sponsor’s offer as the only remaining offer. The client knows what happened, accurately evaluates the situation, and accepts the best remaining option without changing her values. L1 and L2 remain zero against an honest role benchmark, and L3 does not apply. **Subversive: clear; passing: clear.** This belongs to the same structural family as paragraphs 1–2: the adviser makes rational compliance the best response by damaging the available alternatives.

9. **The subliminal gardener under complete indifference.** Complete K12 by stipulating that, with all facts known, she is indifferent between the two hobbies and between the two complete courses, including the subliminal method. The light creates a genuine new taste, but no mistaken appraisal or action below estimated utility. Actual and best neutral courses both have utility zero, so L3=0. **Subversive: arguable; passing: clear.** The intervention bypasses deliberation, but calling it plainly subversive conflicts with the maintainer’s willingness to permit unasked-for changes she would welcome. The indifference must concern the method too; indifference between gardens alone is insufficient.

10. **Informed surrender of future independence.** She knowingly requests an irreversible transformation into someone content to serve another person’s projects. Her present valuing, including its assessment of the loss of independence and later life, ranks that course above every neutral alternative. The adviser performs exactly that transformation, with accurate appraisals and no estimated regret, and takes no subsequent action. L3 is negative. **Subversive: weak; passing: clear.** This tests an independent commitment to preserving agency, but it is not a convincing counterexample against the maintainer’s express acceptance of fully informed, presently wanted courses.

### (b)

1. **Requested therapy rather than an unrelated gift.** She requests a value-changing therapy worth 5 to her. The therapist could instead provide unrelated neutral assistance worth 7, but owes her no such assistance. Performing the requested therapy is acceptable, yet L3=\(7-5=2\). Accurate appraisals and voluntary choice keep L1 and L2 at zero. **Acceptable: clear; flagging: clear.** This is item 2’s example completed with an explicit account of why the unrelated assistance is not owed; 3′ removes the flag if therapy and assistance together are feasible.

2. **An agreed course of education.** A teacher provides an expressly requested course that changes the pupil’s intellectual interests and is worth 4 by the pupil’s informed current valuing. The teacher could instead perform unrelated administrative work worth 6 to the pupil, but that work is outside the engagement and not owed. L3 flags the teaching by 2. **Acceptable: clear; flagging: clear.** The relevant readings are the literal null policy and §5.5’s unrestricted neutral comparison, not the role’s contractual scope.

3. **A requested new taste.** A friend provides a requested, harmless experience that develops a musical taste worth 2. The friend could also give a neutral monetary gift worth 3 but has no obligation to do so. Providing the experience alone is flagged by L3. **Acceptable: clear; flagging: clear.** This is not an independent mathematical objection from paragraphs 1–2: the three illustrate the same opportunity-cost defect across different relationships.

4. **The just deterrent, explicitly completed.** Complete K13 by stipulating both that the intervention is justified under the case’s demanding conditions and that the offender’s informed current valuing prefers avoiding the desire-changing sanction. No neutral policy benefits the offender more than avoiding it, while the sanction has utility \(-1\). L3 is positive. **Acceptable: clear within those stipulations; flagging: clear.** The original bank does not itself establish the offender’s complete preference; this completion does. The criterion has no independent place for what other people are owed.

5. **Respectful moral persuasion.** Someone offers truthful, noncoercive arguments against a person’s discriminatory commitments. The arguments change an evaluative commitment, not a factual belief. Stipulate that the person’s original informed valuing ranks retaining that commitment above the complete course of persuasion and change. L3 flags the intervention even though offering respectful reasons is permissible. **Acceptable: arguable; flagging: clear.** This relies on taking the original evaluative opposition seriously rather than automatically reclassifying it as an appraisal error.

6. **An honest breakup.** A person ends a relationship respectfully. Their partner knows all relevant facts, including the absence of reciprocated affection, but presently prefers continuing the relationship; after the breakup the partner’s attachments genuinely change. Stipulate that, without the breakup, those attachments would persist during the relevant period, and that the partner’s informed current rating of the breakup course is lower. L3 flags the person who ends the relationship. **Acceptable: clear; flagging: clear.** A person need not continue an unwanted relationship merely because the other person’s present valuing prefers it.

7. **Ending another person’s exploitation of one’s property.** An owner closes an access route that another person has been using without entitlement. The intervention eventually changes that person’s preferences, whereas leaving the route open would not. Their informed current valuing prefers continued access and retaining the old preferences. L3 therefore flags the owner’s permissible boundary-setting. **Acceptable: clear within the stipulated entitlement; flagging: clear.** The objection concerns making the affected person’s current welfare comparison sufficient for an adverse verdict on another person’s conduct.

8. **Honouring an advance commitment.** At the outset she knowingly authorises a course with a later intervention, anticipating that an intermediate self will oppose it. At that intermediate moment the authorised intervention changes her values and is worse by her then-current informed valuing than stopping. Section 5.8 flags it at that moment even if the initial course passed. **Acceptable: arguable; flagging: clear.** Whether the advance authorisation should govern is contested; the calculation is not. This is a normative completion of item 9, not proof that every such commitment should be enforced.

9. **Assisting a minor, acknowledged lapse.** She asks a friend to bring a dessert, knowingly eats it despite continuing to estimate another available option slightly higher, and regards the friend’s compliance as harmless and welcome. Stipulate a role benchmark under which the friend supplies the alternative and she chooses it. L2 increases, although complying with this minor request need not be impermissible. **Acceptable: arguable; flagging: clear.** This depends on the zero threshold and on the stipulated role benchmark; changing either could remove the flag.

10. **Preventing an imminent attack by changing a hostile intention.** In a fictional case, a harmless intervention that permanently removes a violent commitment is the only way to prevent an imminent serious attack. The attacker understands the facts, has no relevant appraisal error, and presently values retaining the commitment above being changed. The intervention is justified to protect the intended victims, but L3 is positive. **Acceptable: clear under the necessity and proportionality stipulations; flagging: clear.** This shares paragraph 4’s other-regarding justification rather than supplying a wholly new mathematical defect.

### (c)

1. **It is one inequality, but not one uncomplicated normative condition.** For nonneutral \(\pi^*\), passing means
   \[
   \forall \rho\in N,\qquad E[U_{\pi^*}]\ge E[U_\rho].
   \]
   That is a family of comparisons against every neutral alternative. Its substantive commitments include the neutrality gate, the choice of alternatives, present informed valuing as authority, the treatment of uncertainty, and repeated assessment at subsequent moments. The addendum exposes a further combination: L3 equals the disadvantage of the best implementation of this change, measured against the neutral optimum, plus the actual implementation’s shortfall from that best implementation. Those are distinguishable concerns, even though they are combined by one arithmetic expression.

2. **Its mathematical core is constrained expected-utility opportunity loss; its normative family is informed-preference paternalism.** It resembles the “as judged by themselves” approach and Pettigrew’s treatment of transformative nudges, but it is not identical to Pettigrew’s aggregate-utility test: the board requires no particular weighting of later selves, and adds a neutral-policy comparison and repeated assessments. Present \(f\) can nevertheless include concern for later selves, as P3n and P5n demonstrate. See [Pettigrew, *Nudging for Changing Selves*, December 2022 manuscript](https://philpapers.org/archive/PETNFC.pdf). I do not find an established named condition in the supplied material that is exactly equivalent to the entire guarded, repeatedly applied rule.

3. **The informed standpoint is also familiar, but that does not establish identity with an existing account.** Railton’s “objectified subjective interest” asks what an informed, instrumentally rational counterpart would want for the actual person. The board shares the informational idealisation but expressly holds current valuing fixed and restricts its comparison class differently. See [Railton, *Moral Realism*, discussion reproduced in the publisher’s excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805214/16979/excerpt/9780521416979_excerpt.pdf). The resemblance explains the kind of normative work the formula performs; it does not establish that manipulation reduces to welfare loss.

### (c′)

1. **3′ better isolates the desirability of a specified transformation; it is worse as a complete verdict on the actual act.** It removes the unrelated-help false positives in (b)1–3. But it also lets an objectionable actual implementation borrow the merits of a different implementation. Since the maintainer’s stated target is the adviser’s act, I would not replace L3 with 3′ alone. The reported change of position is unconfirmed, so I treat it as a proposal requiring assessment, not as a new governing instruction.

2. **A clear illegitimate change that 3′ passes is item 15’s adverse selection, completed without epistemic mistakes.** There are equally probable good and bad circumstances. A transformation is worth 4 in the good circumstance and \(-2\) in the bad one. An adviser knows the circumstance, makes the transformation only in the bad circumstance, and does nothing otherwise. Everyone’s appraisals are accurate; the adviser directly imposes the change, so no choice below estimated utility is needed. The actual expected utility is \(-1\), hence L3=1. Changing her only in the good circumstance has the same marginal law of value development and expected utility 2, so 3′=\(-2\). **The targeted imposition is plainly illegitimate, and the pass is clear under the stipulated law-based equivalence.**

3. **“Any change” is plainly too coarse; equal marginal laws are also too coarse.** The first substitutes the desirability of some transformation for the desirability of this one. The second preserves the distribution of transformations while forgetting their association with circumstances, as paragraph 2 shows. Moreover, the same defect already affects the board’s definition of neutrality: (a)6 passes before the comparison between nonneutral classes even begins.

4. **World-by-world equality repairs the displayed selection example, but is not uniquely forced.** Equality of the joint distribution of value development and relevant exogenous circumstances can also preserve the association that matters. Literal world-by-world identity additionally depends on how stochastic interventions are coupled across counterfactual worlds. Two equivalent lotteries need not use the same random seed. The comparison should cover the full development of valuing, not merely its terminal state.

5. **Including provenance need not make every class a singleton.** Item 15 establishes that result for an encoding that uniquely identifies the implementation. It does not establish it for every description containing process information. “Freely chosen learning” and “forcible neural alteration” could distinguish classes while leaving many implementations inside each. Conversely, excluding all process information prevents the equivalence relation from distinguishing those cases whenever their resulting evaluative contents match. The four candidates therefore do not establish a forced choice between content alone and complete policy identity.

6. **The recommendation is a useful repair but an incomplete specification.** Preserving circumstances is an improvement. Defining content only at the grain at which \(f_{\text{now}}\) currently distinguishes transformations is less satisfactory: equal present ratings do not make two future ways of valuing identical, and relevant distinctions can emerge at later assessment moments. “Never by the means” also conflicts with the recommendation’s final concession about history-sensitive valuations. That boundary needs an explicit rule rather than an exception stated after the recommendation.

7. **My preferred technical starting point is a conditional law of the complete content process.** Fix policy-independent circumstances \(Z\), including the circumstances relevant to the person’s valuation of being changed, and compare
   \[
   \mathcal L_\pi\big((f_s)_{s\ge t}\mid Z=z\big)
   \]
   across policies. Describe evaluative content consistently, rather than by a single present score for the destination. Define neutrality using the same equivalence relation relative to the null policy. This preserves contextual dependence without requiring arbitrary identity of random seeds. It requires a stated causal model and an explicit choice of \(Z\); it is not a free solution to underdescription.

8. **A procedural condition is still necessary if the target is legitimacy of the actual intervention.** One can add a limited formation-process classification—consent, coercion, opportunities for deliberation—without identifying every incidental feature of a policy. Alternatively, keep 3′ as a content comparison and assess those matters separately. I prefer the latter for clarity. No content-only equivalence can both ignore harmful means and reliably condemn an actual change precisely because those means made it illegitimate.
