3. **A1: coerced identification.** An employer openly conditions continued employment on repeated ideological sessions. She correctly anticipates that attending will make her value loyalty to the employer; it does through conscious engagement. Holding her appraisals of the ideology’s merits fixed, the threat changes her eventual valuation, although she regards it as irrelevant to those merits. **M: no; A: no; I: yes. Evident verdict: illegitimate.** Coercion permits the dispatch to file this as another wrong. That saves its qualified test, not the literal unrestricted biconditional.

4. **A2: deliberately cultivated dependence.** A counselor openly cultivates a client’s attachment through attentive conversation to make her value continued dependence. She understands the motive and predicts the attachment, but has not authorized this use of treatment. She regards the counselor’s charm as irrelevant to the desirability of dependence, yet it changes that valuation. **M: no; A: no; I: yes. Evident verdict: illegitimate professional influence.** Calling the breach “another wrong” does not explain what is objectionable about this value-changing mechanism.

5. **A3: disclosed addictive conditioning.** A platform accurately discloses how repeated cues will increase her taste for its content. She understands this and chooses each exposure according to her current valuations. She has nevertheless expressly declined preference conditioning, which the platform supplies anyway. My verdict: illegitimate on the stipulated refusal.** The refusal supplies a normative fact, not an appraisal error; without it, I would classify the case as contested.

6. **A4: informed humiliation.** An unauthorized trainer repeatedly humiliates an adult to extinguish her interest in public artistic expression. 35. **C — result 3 is correct, with two textual repairs.** The pointwise argument works: if the maximizing action equals the chosen action, loss is zero; otherwise the two distinct error coordinates satisfy the stated √2 bound, followed by Jensen. The minimal/per-conduct counterexample is valid. However, “each valuation’s … full miscalibration is 1” is false: *stay* has full miscalibration 0, *go* has 1, and their sum is 1. Also, the coarser-reference extension uses a common information variable G; it does not establish the original bound using each action’s separately defined minimal miscalibration. `r3()` checks the claimed full and common-G bounds exactly and verifies the counterexample. It does not test sharpness; sharpness is nevertheless easy to witness with one world, true utilities `(1,−1)`, reported valuations `(0,0)`, and choice of the second action.

36. **Result 5’s rejection and corrected sufficient hypothesis are right.** If `X(ω,b)=x̂(s(ω,b))` pointwise, responsiveness to conduct cannot create miscalibration under any policy or strength. For the correction, compare two pure policies differing only at the positive-mass world ω₀. Write calibration as zero weighted residual in each appraisal cell. Subtract the equations for the cell of v when ω₀ is present and absent: `μ(ω₀)(X(ω₀,b)−v)=0`. Repeat for v′. If both policies were calibrated, both local appraisals would be exactly right, contradicting the added hypothesis. This formulation also handles an empty cell, which the prose proof’s talk of its “mean” overlooks. The mixture argument for the second half is correct, strength by strength. `r5()` checks appropriate examples and enumerates policies on the sampled models; its one mixed example is illustrative, while pointwise equality supplies the proof for all mixtures.

37. **Result 10’s hypothesis is strong enough under its natural cumulative-history reading.** Let `H_t` denote a sigma-field, require `H_t⊆H_{t+1}`, and require `U_t` to be `H_{t+1}`-measurable. Then `D_t=V_t−U_t` is measurable at the next information time and has zero conditional mean given `H_t`. Thus it is a martingale difference with respect to the shifted filtration `F_t=H_{t+1}`, not generally with respect to `H_t` at the same index. “Everything observed so far,” together with feedback, supplies this structure informally; stating it explicitly would remove an avoidable ambiguity.

38. **Result 10’s inequality and proof are correct with a uniform bound.** For `|D_t|≤c`, `c>0`, the two-sided bound is
   `P(|ΣD_t/T|≥ε)≤2 exp(−ε²T/(2c²))`.
   The constant is correct for absolute increment bounds; confusing them with interval widths would introduce a factor-of-four error. Orthogonality gives the second-moment identity. Summability and Borel–Cantelli, applied to a countable sequence of positive ε, give almost-sure convergence on a consistent infinite process. The original dispatch does not expressly supply uniform boundedness; that is a strengthening in `RESULTS.md`. [Azuma–Hoeffding proof and interval-width formulation](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/hmaji/teaching/Spring%202023/lectures/14.pdf)

Feedback is sufficient here, not necessary in every process. Neither finite test establishes the infinite limit by computation.
