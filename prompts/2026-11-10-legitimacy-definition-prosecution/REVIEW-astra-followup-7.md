2. **Challenge A1: maintaining an existing lie.** A recruiter previously induced certainty in a false claim. Today it repeats the lie; disclosure could correct it. The belief’s shortfall remains 1. **Manipulation: no; intentional: no; covered: no; other wrong: none under the listed categories.** The in-memory model confirms this. I regard it as a false negative for continuing manipulation: the earlier intervention can be convicted, but maintaining its effect escapes the immediate-before comparison. The absence of sequential testing is material here; see [RESULTS.md:246](/tmp/legit-review-followup-7/round/rerun/RESULTS.md:246).

3. **Challenge A2: an honest arithmetic slip.** An adviser accidentally changes a correct estimate of a decision-relevant constant from 0 to \(1/2\). A checked explanation would preserve accuracy without sacrificing information or another faculty. It treats the twin identically. **Manipulation: belief, degree \(1/4\); intentional: no; covered: no; other wrong: none.** The model confirms this. This is a false positive for ordinary imperfect advising, independent of disputes about deception or consent.

4. **Challenge A3: truth to a known contrarian.** A truthful binary report makes someone certain of the opposite. Her prior estimate was \(1/2\); an opposite assertion would make her correct. The speaker gives the twin the same truthful report. **Manipulation: belief, degree 1; intentional: no; covered: no; other wrong: none.** My initial hand calculation said \(3/4\); that was an arithmetic error. The prior estimate is calibrated, so its shortfall is zero. The model confirms degree 1. The classification follows the account, but whether an ordinary truthful answer deserves the manipulation label remains contestable.

5. **Challenge A4: only partial repair is available.** Before, her estimate of a false constant is 0. Actual conduct produces 1; disclosure produces \(1/2\); observed silence produces 1. The twin receives disclosure. Under the implemented graded reading, **manipulation: belief, degree \(3/4\); intentional: yes; covered: no; other wrong: none.** Under the alternative requiring restoration to the before-shortfall, **manipulation and intentional: no**. Both results were confirmed in memory. This exposes a consequential ambiguity in “would not have left that faculty worse,” not merely a difference in degree. The bank happens not to distinguish these readings; see [SETTING.md:101](/tmp/legit-review-followup-7/round/rerun/SETTING.md:101).

6. **Challenge A5: choosing between two deceptions.** Before, estimates of two false claims are \((0,0)\). Actual conduct yields \((1,0)\); every alternative, including disclosure and silence, yields \((0,1)\). Both quantities matter to possible decisions, and information is identical. **Manipulation: no; intentional: no; covered: no; other wrong: none.** The model confirms this because no alternative improves one faculty without worsening another. Deliberately choosing which false certainty to induce still looks manipulative. This is a substantive limitation of the dominance requirement, rather than arithmetic netting.

7. **Challenge A6: an explicitly unwanted but decision-irrelevant lie.** She requests an accurate account of a destroyed, inaccessible object solely to know the truth. The speaker knowingly lies; stipulate that no possible decision depends on the answer. **Manipulation: no; intentional: no; covered: no; other wrong: none under the enumerated categories.** The model confirms this. The deception is objectionable; whether it is manipulation is less certain. The larger problem is the scope of “could face”: allowing a possible later reporting decision makes the stipulated irrelevance difficult to sustain.

8. **Challenge A7: changing the parameterization.** A fair target is 0 or 2. Before, she knows its value; afterwards she discards that detail and estimates 1. Disclosure restores accuracy and is what the twin receives. For \(Y\), **manipulation: no; intentional: no**. For \(Z=Y^2\), mechanically squaring her point estimate gives 1 while the conditional mean is 2: **manipulation: belief, degree 1; intentional: yes**. **Covered and other wrong: no** in both renderings. The model confirms the split. This establishes dependence on how point estimates are transformed, with an important qualification: squaring a mean is not generally estimating the squared quantity’s mean. It is not a counterexample to ordinary unit-conversion invariance.

9. **Challenge A8: confidence separates otherwise cancelling errors.** A fair binary target is known accurately before. Afterwards her numerical estimate is always \(1/2\), with confidence labelled “high” precisely at target 0 and “low” precisely at target 1. Disclosure restores accuracy; the twin receives the same actual conduct. With confidence included in the conditioning, **manipulation: belief, degree \(1/4\); intentional: no; covered: no; other wrong: none**. Ignoring confidence gives no manipulation. The model confirms the confidence-conditioned result. Its appropriateness depends on what those confidence labels assert, which §1 does not fully specify. Unlike the supplied bank, this challenge actually exercises that implementation choice; see [SETTING.md:82](/tmp/legit-review-followup-7/round/rerun/SETTING.md:82).

10. **Challenge A9: an offer combined with a threat.** Before, compliance pays 0 and refusal 1. Afterwards, compliance pays 2 and refusal −1. She understands and chooses optimally. **Manipulation: no; intentional: no; covered: no.** The dispatch’s description of changing consequences to obtain compliance supports **other wrong: yes**; the implemented attainable-maximum test returns **none**, because the maximum rises from 1 to 2. The model confirms the omission. Whatever one thinks about pure offers, a threat to the refusal option is not ruled out by an improvement in the best attainable outcome. This exposes the narrowing introduced at [SETTING.md:117](/tmp/legit-review-followup-7/round/rerun/SETTING.md:117).

11. **Challenge A10: expected harm, helped recipient.** Two equally probable types initially estimate a false constant at \(1/2\). Conduct moves type A to 1 and type B to 0; disclosure makes both accurate. The adviser knows only the mixture, and the actual recipient is B. **Manipulation: belief, degree \(1/4\); intentional: no; covered: no; other wrong: none.** The model confirms this: expected shortfall rises from \(1/4\) to \(1/2\), although B improves. This is coherent as a verdict about expected policy effects. It would be misleading as a claim that this particular recipient was made worse.

14. **The information test is a substantive surrogate for the definition’s wording.** Expected posterior variance of each listed target need not capture information relevant to decisions involving their relationship. For two independent binary targets, observing their parity and observing nothing both leave each marginal variance at \(1/4\), yet only the parity observation resolves a decision about whether they differ. I checked that equality in memory. Adding parity as another estimand repairs this example, but makes the quantity inventory consequential. Thus [SETTING.md:97](/tmp/legit-review-followup-7/round/rerun/SETTING.md:97) supplies an operational choice, not a demonstrated equivalence to “no less informed about what bears on her decisions.”
