# Research-program context

The deference line asks when and how a bounded agent can defer to another agent without simply surrendering final authority.

The motivating thesis is roughly:

> Epistemic superiority need not entail final authority.

The longer-term target is a meaningful **corrigibility theorem**: a theorem explaining when deference to a stronger reasoner preserves some relevant capacity of the principal to remain authoritative, correct, revise, recover, or avoid being foreclosed upon.

The workspace has learned several important negative lessons.

## 1. Logical-Induction deference

Earlier work studied relations among a slower/principal inductor, a faster/advisory inductor, and updated or augmented agents.

The line includes notions such as:

- Total Trust;
- Value;
- Tower-like relations;
- faithful acceleration;
- delayed prediction / later computation;
- cross-agent calibration and forcing;
- deference to another inductor;
- successor/future-agent constructions.

A standing issue has been separating algebraic implications from genuine consequences of the Logical Induction Criterion.

The June corpus’s own audit found that several forcing results imported their crucial forcing inequalities as hypotheses rather than deriving them from an explicit market/trader construction.

The workspace therefore opened `PRIORITIES.md` items 7–9 around:

- modelling the actual market and traders;
- the doubly-soft weight class;
- forcing headlines that were squeezes over hypotheses too close to the conclusion.

Subsequent workspace work partially closed item 7 by building an actual FAF trader/criterion-to-signed-forcing chain, while leaving cross-process residue.

## 2. Corrigibility / successor work

The workspace then tried to move from epistemic deference to a richer corrigibility question.

Several comparator constructions failed for a structural reason:

> Two authorization regimes that induce the same realization map are indistinguishable in a type whose only semantically relevant output is that realization map.

This led to a type-level diagnosis: corrigibility requires representing something like authorization, jurisdiction, corrective capacity, or future control explicitly rather than merely decorating an ordinary choice function.

The current workspace therefore distinguishes:

- epistemic/value deference;
- execution;
- authority/jurisdiction;
- future corrective capability;
- foreclosure.

`PRIORITIES.md` Q3 currently asks how foreclosure should even be expressed.

## 3. Cartesian Frames bridge

A recent round explored Cartesian Frames as a candidate language for representing what is lost when future corrective agency is restricted or transferred.

That round produced useful candidate objects but did **not** solve Q3:

- a frame has no intrinsic time coordinate;
- the relevant authorization relation is still missing;
- “future” remains supplied externally by the modeller;
- transfer/restriction structures represent counterfactual control better than entitlement.

So this is promising interface work, not yet the corrigibility theorem.

## 4. Current epistemic discipline

The workspace distinguishes sharply among:

- inherited/source-corpus claims;
- model-generated prose;
- kernel-verified declarations;
- registered workspace claims;
- genuinely `workspace-established` results.

Do not collapse those distinctions.

A theorem being present in Abram's corpus does **not** automatically make it a workspace-established result.

A theorem being kernel-checked in the imported corpus does **not** automatically make it registered in this repository.
